# AI Impostor Phenomenon

## What It Is

Feeling fraudulent after AI-assisted success because the result is difficult to attribute to one's own abilities. This is an application of the established impostor phenomenon to workplace AI augmentation, not a clinical diagnosis or proof that the worker lacks competence.

## Why It Matters

A better result need not feel like a personal achievement. A person can contribute real value and still struggle to internalize success when AI supplied capabilities they cannot reproduce independently. That reaction may affect willingness to share knowledge or help colleagues.

## Key Insight

In Zhao and He's (2024) short proceedings report, randomized AI access during a writing task increased impostor thoughts; recall and scenario studies examined intrinsic motivation as a moderator. Workers who valued doing the activity for its own sake generally reported a stronger effect.

A separate three-wave survey connected AI augmentation with lower self-reported knowledge sharing and helping through impostor thoughts. That downstream pathway is an association, not an experimental demonstration of harm to colleagues.

## Evidence Boundaries

The proceedings paper contains brief study reports, incomplete attrition information, and an inconsistent statement about one subgroup's statistical significance. The AI-specific evidence therefore remains emerging. The studies did not test a remedy or establish that reduced confidence reflects actual skill loss.

This differs from [[professional-identity-threat]], which concerns the broader sense of being a professional or creator, and [[ai-self-efficacy-erosion]], which concerns belief in one's ability to act. It also differs from the [[confidence-competence-gap]]: impostor feelings can occur despite real competence.

[Inference] The related [[competence-authenticity]] construct concerns perceived alignment of outcomes with personal mastery, understanding, and effort. AI impostor feelings describe the narrower experience of fraudulence after success, rather than every question about the origin of a result. The distinction is interpretive; neither study tests the constructs' empirical independence.
