# Identity-Threat Coping

## Overview

A proposed vocabulary for discussing five compensatory responses to GenAI-related concerns about competence, autonomy and relatedness. Hermann, Puntoni & Morewedge (2025) adapt it from self-discrepancy research. It is a discussion aid, not a validated diagnostic instrument or intervention.

## What It Is / How It Works

Hermann, Puntoni & Morewedge (2025) adapt five strategies from the self-discrepancy and identity-threat literatures:

1. **Direct resolution** — Behavior that resolves the source of the discrepancy. Examples: enrolling in skill-building training, adapting workflows to incorporate GenAI effectively, learning prompt engineering. Addresses the threat at its root but requires time, effort, and often organizational support.

2. **Symbolic self-completion** — Behavior that signals mastery in the domain of the discrepancy without resolving it. Examples: emphasizing credentials, titles, or human-only domain expertise in communications; foregrounding "what GenAI can't do that I can" in self-presentation. It may support self-perception without resolving the underlying concern; effects depend on context.

3. **Dissociation** — Behavior that separates oneself from the domain where the discrepancy is triggered. Examples: avoiding GenAI-dependent tasks, withdrawing psychologically from areas where GenAI exposes skill gaps, reducing identification with threatened parts of the role. It may provide relief or reflect a reasonable boundary; costs and benefits depend on the circumstances.

4. **Escapism** — Behavior that distracts from thinking about the discrepancy. Examples: extended breaks, requesting reassignment, disengaging from work tasks, browsing unrelated content during work hours. The article does not establish a universal ranking of this response or a GenAI-specific causal effect on exhaustion and performance.

5. **Fluid compensation** — Behavior that reinforces other, unthreatened aspects of identity. Examples: a marketer threatened by GenAI on content creation shifting identification toward campaign strategy; a developer threatened by code generation emphasizing system architecture or mentorship. It may help when the alternative domain is meaningful and available; the taxonomy does not establish its effectiveness for a particular worker.

The taxonomy maps onto the three basic psychological needs (Hermann et al., Table 3 [p.8]):

| Strategy | Competence threat | Autonomy threat | Relatedness threat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct resolution | Skill enhancement, workflow adaptation | Workflow adjustment to preserve final-decision authority | Collaboration with colleagues to learn GenAI together |
| Symbolic self-completion | Emphasis on irreplaceable human expertise | Emphasis on ownership and leadership in GenAI tasks | Emphasis on human-driven team contributions |
| Dissociation | Avoid GenAI-dependent tasks | Avoid GenAI-automated tasks | Prefer human-human teamwork |
| Escapism | Mental withdrawal, delegation to others | Extended breaks, reassignment requests | Isolation from GenAI-prominent settings |
| Fluid compensation | Shift to uniquely human skill domains | Shift to roles where decisions remain human-driven | Shift to customer-facing or human-centred roles |

## What To Do

[Inference] The following questions adapt the taxonomy for a conversation, not a diagnosis. The source does not test this procedure or establish which response is best for a particular worker.

For an organization or manager observing GenAI-related strain in workers:

1. **Ask which need may be under strain** — competence, autonomy, or relatedness. Discussion question: what does the worker say they have lost? Capability and effectiveness (competence), control and self-direction (autonomy), or connection and belonging (relatedness)?

2. **Ask whether any strategy describes the worker's experience** — direct resolution, symbolic self-completion, dissociation, escapism, or fluid compensation. Possible examples, not diagnostic signs: enrolment in training (1), prominence of credentials in communications (2), avoidance behaviours and reduced engagement in GenAI-mediated work (3), withdrawal patterns and rising absenteeism (4), shift in stated identity to alternative domain (5).

3. **Discuss costs and benefits in this context.** Avoid labelling a response productive or maladaptive from its category alone. Consider the worker's account, available options, job demands and organizational constraints.

4. **Consider support that addresses the stated concern.** Options might include training time, role clarification, task redesign or greater choice. These are proposals to discuss and evaluate, not interventions validated by the taxonomy.

5. **Check whether the chosen response is feasible.** Training needs time and access; a proposed alternative role must be available and valued. Support should follow the worker's needs, not a presumed ranking of strategies.

## Rationale and Evidence Limits

The five strategies are not idiosyncratic — they are documented across self-discrepancy research (Stinson et al., cited by Hermann et al.). What is novel here is the application to GenAI-induced workplace threats and the explicit mapping to the three basic psychological needs. Self-determination theory has strong empirical support (Ryan & Deci, 2017; Van den Broeck et al., 2016 meta-analysis), so the underlying need-frustration framing is well-grounded.

The taxonomy provides vocabulary, not a validated diagnosis or prediction. Hermann et al.'s Outstanding Questions (p.9) explicitly call for research on which factors shape responses and their consequences. General support for self-determination theory does not validate this GenAI-specific application.

## Strengths / Limitations

**Strengths:**
- Names patterns the KB has been describing piecemeal (reactance, withdrawal, upskilling, identity shift) into a coherent five-strategy vocabulary.
- Organizes discussion around three needs without establishing an individual diagnosis.
- Encourages discussion of possible costs and benefits; comparative effectiveness remains untested.
- Suggests questions about organizational support, not proven intervention effects.

**Limitations:**
- The taxonomy is imported from self-discrepancy theory and applied analogically — Hermann et al. provide no GenAI-specific empirical demonstrations of any of the five strategies operating in workplace contexts. Treat as theoretical framework, not validated diagnostic tool.
- The five strategies are not necessarily mutually exclusive or exhaustive. A worker may simultaneously use direct resolution and symbolic self-completion; a sixth pattern (e.g., collective bargaining as in the Hollywood Writers' strike) may not fit the individual-level frame.
- Does not establish which strategy is adaptive or which a worker will choose. Possible individual and organizational moderators require testing.
- Responses such as dissociation or escapism may reflect organizational conditions rather than an individual deficit — the method risks pathologizing workers who are responding rationally to under-supportive environments.

Wang and Zhou (2025) provide adjacent, not validating, evidence: in a cross-sectional survey of Chinese university faculty, replacement concerns, perceived organizational support, commitment and burnout were associated. The study did not test these five coping strategies or any support intervention. [Inference] Assess the workplace resources and credible role threats before interpreting a worker’s response as an individual coping problem (PDF pp.4–10).

## When It Applies

- Organizations introducing GenAI tools into established workflows where worker resistance, withdrawal, or identity-protection behaviours are visible.
- Managers discussing concerns with workers without presuming that resistance indicates a personal deficit.
- Workers themselves examining their own responses to GenAI-induced threats and considering whether their current strategy is productive in their context.

When NOT to use:
- As a substitute for engaging with the substantive concern. If a worker says GenAI is eroding their craft, classifying that as "symbolic self-completion" or "fluid compensation" without examining whether the concern is empirically valid is reductive.
- As a predictive tool. The framework offers possible descriptions; it neither establishes a worker's motives nor reliably predicts what they will do next.
