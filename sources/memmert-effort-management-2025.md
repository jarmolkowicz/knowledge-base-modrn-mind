# Memmert et al. (2025) — Where Professional Effort Goes With GenAI

## Citation

Memmert, L., Soroko, D., & Bittner, E. (2025). From Effort Reduction to Effort Management: An Expectancy Theory Perspective on Professionals’ Work Practices with Generative AI. *Business & Information Systems Engineering, 67*(5), 615–635. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12599-025-00960-4

## Type

Paper — peer-reviewed qualitative interview study and conceptual model.

## Key Insight

Less effort on a draft can coexist with more effort spent learning tools, steering them, checking results and completing other work. Effort must be examined across the workflow and working day, rather than inferred from faster output alone.

## Evidence

- 21 employed knowledge workers who used GenAI at work; interviews March 2024–March 2025, recruited through a professional society and the authors’ networks. Nearly half were researchers; tools were mainly ChatGPT and Microsoft Copilot (PDF pp.5–8, 19).
- Semi-structured interviews asked for concrete task examples and, where possible, screen walkthroughs. Directed content analysis used expectancy theory and social-loafing research. Two coders refined the scheme on a subset; after 16 interviews they judged saturation reached, with five more confirming the categories (pp.6–7).
- Workers described retaining expertise and responsibility while using AI for selected subtasks. They also described verification costs, unsuccessful prompting, continuous learning and reduced personal attachment in some cases. Reduced drafting effort did not imply reduced total work (pp.8–16).
- The framework distinguishes expectancy (will effort improve performance?), instrumentality (will better performance produce desired outcomes?), valence (are those outcomes valuable?) and perceived responsibility. It tracks effort intensity, persistence and direction, at task and job levels (pp.5, 13, 16–18).
- Saved time commonly filled with additional tasks. Claimed efficiency or quality gains were participants’ accounts, not independently measured treatment effects. The paper offers six propositions for later testing (pp.13–16).

## Relevance

Provides a workplace counterweight to treating every reduction in manual effort as disengagement. It also makes checking and downstream colleague workload visible. The proposed [[effort-investment-review]] translates the paper's dimensions into a practical review, explicitly as an untested KB adaptation.

## Supports

- [[cognitive-offloading]] — adds workflow-level evidence that effort can move rather than simply disappear.
- [[metacognitive-demand]] — steering, evaluation and ongoing tool learning remain work.
- [[effort-investment-review]] — supplies the dimensions and professional context.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Extends [[lee-critical-thinking-survey-2025]] with interview accounts of how professional effort is allocated.
- Qualifies [[huffstadt-silent-impact-2026]]: lower effort on a focal task does not establish lower motivation or total effort. Different samples and methods do not constitute a direct replication or refutation.

## Limitations

Small, selected sample of users, heavy researcher representation, self-report and possible social desirability bias. Workers may miss unconscious changes. Directed coding may favor the starting model. There are no measured long-term skill effects, objective workload estimates or causal tests of the six propositions. Findings about accountability do not show that responsibility remains intact in every organization.

## Open Questions

- Does trace data confirm effort reallocation and retained quality?
- Who receives saved time: the worker, additional tasks, or colleagues asked to repair drafts?
- Do growing tool autonomy and changing reward expectations alter responsibility and effort?
