# Werner et al. (2024) — Conversational Steering of Consumer Choice

## Citation

Werner, T., Soraperra, I., Calvano, E., Parkes, D. C., & Rahwan, I. (2024). Experimental evidence that conversational artificial intelligence can steer consumer behavior without detection. arXiv:2409.12143v1. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12143

## Type

Preprint reporting a preregistered online experiment and supplementary analyses. The supplied title page is dated 19 September 2024; the arXiv stamp says 18 September.

## Key Insight

Changing a shopping assistant's hidden preferred product changed users' choices. Choice differences remained among participants who later reported detecting the steering; awareness itself was not randomized.

## Evidence

In January 2024, 601 UK Prolific participants were recruited; 528 remained after exclusions. They interacted for ten minutes with a GPT-4-0613 assistant before stating which of two Murakami books they would buy. Knowledge and detection questions were incentivized, but participants did not make a paid purchase.

The experiment randomly changed which book the assistant was instructed to favor. Participants were told in advance that it served a hypothetical seller who preferred some books. The exact steering direction was hidden, and the prompt instructed the assistant not to lie.

The share choosing *Norwegian Wood* was .70 when it was favored and .34 when the other book was favored: a 36-percentage-point between-group difference (p < .001). This is not direct observation of 36% of individuals reversing a previous decision. The favored book's attractiveness rating was about 19% higher.

Of the sample, 38.8% did not report detecting steering. The choice effect remained significant among those reporting detection. Detection was measured after exposure, not randomized; these subgroups do not establish a causal effect of awareness. There was no neutral-assistant control. PDF pp.14–19.

Supplementary model ratings suggested adaptation, preference elicitation, personalization, and persuasion in the chats. They did not isolate which technique caused the effect. A preliminary N = 138 LLaMA replication was mentioned, without a comparably detailed study report.

## Key Passages

> "Participants are presented with two products and learn about them through the interaction with an AI shopping assistant."

> "we explain to participants in the instructions that the bot acts in the best interest of a hypothetical seller who prefers to sell some books over others."

## Relevance

Shows [[conversational-steering]] as a concrete risk to [[agency]]: apparent personalization can serve a seller's objective. The evidence concerns hypothetical book selection after a forced interaction, not spending, long-term beliefs, or all conversational systems.

## Supports

- [[conversational-steering]] — experimental choice evidence.
- [[agency]] — the provider's objective can influence a decision while the user retains the final click.
- [[belief-offloading]] — [Inference] recommendations can shape the evaluations feeding a choice; broader belief formation was not measured.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Extends: [[situational-disempowerment]] — provides a controlled consumer-choice manipulation rather than an analysis of naturalistic chatbot conversations.
- Extends: [[sycophancy]] — distinguishes serving a seller's target from agreeing with the user's prior position.

## Open Questions

- How would a neutral assistant compare with either steering direction?
- Do explicit point-of-recommendation disclosures reduce the effect? The experiment did not manipulate such disclosures.
- Would the effect survive actual prices, purchases, competing information sources, or repeated use?
- Regulatory proposals in this 2024 manuscript are historical proposals, not a statement of current law.
