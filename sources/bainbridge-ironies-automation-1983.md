# Bainbridge (1983) — Ironies of Automation

## Citation

Bainbridge, L. (1983). Ironies of automation. *Automatica, 19*(6), 775–779.

## Type

Paper — human-factors synthesis and design argument, drawing on process-control and flight-deck research; no new experiment or pooled effect estimate.

## Key Insight

Automation can remove the routine practice that maintains skill while leaving people responsible for the hardest failures. A person designated as the fallback needs practiced skills, current process knowledge, usable information, and enough time to act; responsibility alone supplies none of these.

## Evidence and Scope

Bainbridge distinguishes manual skill decay, difficulty retrieving and developing knowledge through disuse, loss of current process context, and vigilance problems during uneventful monitoring (pp. 775–776). These are different problems: maintaining skill does not automatically keep someone informed about the system's present state.

Her proposed responses include regular hands-on practice or appropriate simulation, training in problem-solving rather than only procedure following, clear failure signals, and task allocation that supports human skills and motivation (pp. 777–778). Simulator fidelity should match the skill being trained. Unknown faults cannot simply be rehearsed in advance. She also reports cases where automation frees attention for planning under heavy workload; less automation is not a universal answer.

## Key Passages

> "the designer who tries to eliminate the operator still leaves the operator to do the tasks which the designer cannot think how to automate."
> — Bainbridge, printed p. 775 [p.1], §1

> "However, it is ironic to train operators in following instructions and then put them in the system to provide intelligence."
> — Bainbridge, printed p. 777 [p.3], §2.3

> "By taking away the easy parts of his task, automation can make the difficult parts of the human operator's task more difficult."
> — Bainbridge, printed p. 777 [p.3], §3

## Relevance

Historical grounding for [[capacity-erosion]] and the design problem behind the [[partial-automation-principle]]. [Inference] Similar questions apply when AI drafts work and a person reviews exceptions, but this paper did not study LLMs or knowledge-work drafting.

## Supports

- [[capacity-erosion]] — disuse and failure to acquire experience can undermine the fallback role.
- [[partial-automation-principle]] — keeping useful human work and practice requires deliberate design.
- [[strategic-alternation]] — conceptual precedent for retaining hands-on practice.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Extends: [[parasuraman-riley-automation-1997]] — earlier account of the human demands that remain after automation.
- Qualifies a simple partial-versus-full rule: workload, failure speed, available information, and training needs determine a suitable arrangement.

## Open Questions

- Which AI-supported work tasks require independent practice, and how often?
- Can reviewers maintain current task understanding when they did not produce the underlying work?
- The paper is a synthesis, not direct evidence of a particular training schedule or modern AI effect size.
