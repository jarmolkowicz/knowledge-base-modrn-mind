# Risko & Gilbert (2016) — Cognitive Offloading

## Citation

Risko, E. F., & Gilbert, S. J. (2016). Cognitive offloading. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 20(9), 676–688.

**DOI:** [10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002)

## Type

Paper (review)

## Key Insight

Cognitive offloading — using physical action or external tools to reduce cognitive demand — is ancient (finger-counting, knots in handkerchiefs) but only recently studied systematically. Risko & Gilbert advance two arguments about offloading:

1. **The decision to offload is driven by metacognitive evaluation, not just objective demand** — and those metacognitive evaluations can be wrong. People offload even when it doesn't help performance (and sometimes when it hurts), because they *believe* it will.
2. **Offloading can change subsequent cognition and self-assessment.** The review describes task-specific effects of internet search, navigation aids and photographing objects, with both benefits and costs. It proposes a three-arrow model: (A) metacognitive evaluation → strategy selection, (B) strategy use → updated metacognition, (C) strategy use → direct effect on cognition.

The model predicts a **self-reinforcing shift away from reliance on internal capabilities** in environments with effective cognitive technologies. Increased reliance is not the same as demonstrated capability loss. The GPS and camera examples concern particular learning and memory tasks; the review leaves long-term consequences open.

## Key Passages

> "Cognitive offloading: the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task so as to reduce cognitive demand."
> — Risko & Gilbert, [p.1] (Glossary)

> "Propensity to offload cognition is influenced by the internal cognitive demands that would otherwise be necessary. It is also influenced by metacognitive evaluations of our mental abilities. These metacognitive evaluations are potentially erroneous, which may lead to suboptimal offloading behavior."
> — Risko & Gilbert, [p.1]

> "Individuals who had recently used Google to help them complete a quiz reported higher levels of cognitive self-esteem. They also predicted that they would do better on a subsequent quiz, even without help from external resources."
> — Risko & Gilbert, [p.7] (citing Ward, 2013, and Wegner & Ward, 2013; references 50–51)

> "The model predicts a type of self-reinforcing pattern that will produce a drift away from reliance on internal capabilities when situated in an environment with effective cognitive technologies."
> — Risko & Gilbert, [p.10]

> "Beyond reducing cognitive demand, offloading could also qualitatively change the processes involved in thinking, communicating, and learning, potentially with both positive and negative consequences."
> — Risko & Gilbert, [p.9]

## Relevance

A foundational review of [[cognitive-offloading]], not a study of generative AI.

- Metacognitive evaluations influence whether people offload; those evaluations can be inaccurate.
- Offloading can improve immediate performance while changing what is remembered or how people assess their knowledge. Effects depend on the task and form of assistance.
- [Inference] The model offers questions for GenAI use, including whether delegation replaces learning opportunities. It does not show that GenAI always inflates confidence or causes lasting skill erosion.

[Inference] [[strategic-alternation]], [[think-first]] and [[calibration]] are possible applications, not interventions validated by this review.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Later tested by [[hu-metamemory-offloading-2019]] — Hu et al. provide later experimental confirmation of the metacognitive-evaluation mechanism Risko & Gilbert propose theoretically.
- Developed further in [[gilbert-intention-offloading-2023]] — Gilbert (the same author, with collaborators) operationalizes intention offloading more rigorously in this 2023 paper.

## Open Questions

- Risko & Gilbert flag long-term effects of pervasive offloading as "a particularly pressing concern… for researchers and society in general."
- Different offloading targets (paper note vs. computer note vs. internet vs. human collaborator) — are they cognitively equivalent? The paper says no clear answer yet.
- How do offloading propensities develop across the lifespan? Most studies are on adults.
