# Autonomy-Preserving AI Design

## Overview

A design framework from Buijsman, Carter and Bermúdez (2025) for protecting domain-specific autonomy when AI supports decisions. It addresses competence during use, competence over time, and whether changes in a person's values remain open to reflection and endorsement.

## What It Is / How It Works

The authors propose four responses: make failures easier to recognize; allocate substantive work to people; provide independent skill practice; and support reflection on values through positive friction and systems sensitive to different values (pp. 11–18). This is a philosophical framework informed by prior studies, not a validated intervention package.

## What To Do

[Inference] This untested checklist adapts the authors' four recommendations and adds an initial scoping question:

1. Identify the decisions and capabilities people need to retain. Losing a minor task skill need not reduce their wider autonomy.
2. Provide usable reasons to doubt an output: warnings about unusual inputs, missing context, or evidence for another conclusion. Do not assume an explanation or confidence score guarantees reliable warning.
3. Give people work requiring their own reasoning, with permission to disagree. A nominal override can be meaningless when the organization penalizes its use.
4. Where AI and people work on the same task, provide regular independent practice in the skills needed to assess and correct the result. Set frequency according to the task; the paper specifies no tested interval.
5. Make relevant assumptions and value choices open to reflection. Use friction selectively and account for cultural and personal differences; evaluate the privacy and bias costs of personalization.

## Why It Works

The proposed logic is that failure signals support [[metacognition]], substantive tasks and practice sustain competence, and reflection helps users notice and endorse value changes. The authors explicitly identify evidence gaps for training and positive-friction measures. Effectiveness must be tested in the setting where the framework is used.

## Strengths / Limitations

- Covers system behavior, task allocation, training, and organizational constraints together.
- Distinguishes competence from value ownership rather than treating all delegation as loss of [[agency]].
- Does not supply validated measures, a universal practice schedule, or proof that added friction protects values.
- Does not replace domain-specific professional or safety requirements.

## When It Applies

For teams designing AI decision-support workflows, particularly where people remain responsible for evaluating outcomes. Use as a design review, followed by local testing of both performance and retained human capability.
