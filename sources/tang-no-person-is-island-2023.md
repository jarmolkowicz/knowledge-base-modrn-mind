# Tang et al. (2023) — Social Needs After Workplace AI Interaction

## Citation

Tang, P. M., Koopman, J., Mai, K. M., De Cremer, D., Zhang, J. H., Reynders, P., Ng, C. T. S., & Chen, I.-H. (2023). No Person Is an Island: Unpacking the Work and After-Work Consequences of Interacting With Artificial Intelligence. *Journal of Applied Psychology*. https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0001103

## Type

Paper — peer-reviewed, multi-study research: one field survey, two field experiments and one online simulation. Studies were not preregistered.

## Key Insight

Workplace AI interaction can increase both the desire for human connection and loneliness. These states connect to different outcomes: affiliation-seeking to helping colleagues, and loneliness to poorer after-work well-being. The indirect pathways should not be mistaken for uniform overall increases in helping, drinking or insomnia.

## Evidence

- Study 1: 166 Taiwanese biomedical engineers, three weekly waves; employee reports of AI use and social states, coworker reports of helping, family reports of drinking and insomnia. This is observational (PDF pp.6–7).
- Study 2: 120 Indonesian real-estate consultants randomly instructed to use AI as much as possible or avoid it for three days. Study 4: the same intervention with 294 Malaysian technology-company employees, coworker-rated helping and self-reported after-work outcomes. Control participants still reported some AI interaction, so conditions changed frequency rather than guaranteeing zero use (pp.8–9, 13–14).
- Study 3: 214 US working adults in an online lemonade-business simulation, with or without a scripted advisory interaction using Amazon Polly. Outcomes were intentions to help/drink and expected sleep difficulty, not subsequent observed behavior (pp.10–13).
- Total N=794. All four studies linked AI interaction to greater affiliation need and loneliness. Estimated indirect helping paths through affiliation need were positive; indirect insomnia paths through loneliness were positive. The alcohol pathway was nonsignificant in Study 2 (indirect effect .558, 95% CI [−.209, 1.380]) (pp.7, 9, 12, 14).
- Attachment anxiety generally strengthened paths, but did not significantly moderate AI-to-loneliness in Study 1. Study 4 illustrates competing paths: loneliness also predicted less helping, and affiliation need predicted less insomnia. Significant indirect effects are not the same as positive total effects (pp.7, 14–18).

## Relevance

Adds workplace AI systems and after-work outcomes to [[ai-loneliness-effect]]. The studies collected data in 2021–2022 and are not trials of modern generative companions. [Inference] Workplace evaluation should track social experience as well as task output.

## Supports

- [[ai-loneliness-effect]] — extends social-risk evidence beyond companion use, while preserving the difference in systems and study periods.
- [[social-friction]] — [Inference] human contact has functions that an efficient advisory interaction may not provide; corrective disagreement itself was not measured here.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Extends [[fang-ai-loneliness-2025]] with earlier workplace settings, different AI systems and affiliation-seeking outcomes. It is complementary evidence, not a direct replication of a chatbot dose-response curve.
- Helping can be an attempt to repair a social deficit. It is not evidence that AI has fulfilled the person's need for connection.

## Limitations

Short interventions, reported outcomes, simultaneous measurement of mediators and downstream measures in experiments, and no randomized mediation test. Study 3 measured intentions and had limited realism. Studies 1–2 required cohabiting family. The proposed unmet-social-expectations mechanism was not separated from less coworker interaction. Generalization to current conversational models or long-term mental health requires new evidence.

## Open Questions

- Do repeated workplace interactions create lasting effects or adaptation?
- Which forms of human contact protect social connection without removing useful AI support?
- Some prose overstates total outcomes and contains numerical-label inconsistencies: p.7 says a conditional interval includes zero while printing [.033, .158]. Use the reported interval and distinguish indirect from total effects.
