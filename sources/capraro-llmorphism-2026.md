# Capraro — LLMorphism

## Citation

Capraro, V. (2026). *LLMorphism: When humans come to see themselves as language models*. Preprint, [arXiv:2605.05419v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05419v1), submitted 6 May 2026. The primary record matches the supplied title, author, abstract and 16-page length; the preprint record does not establish peer review.

## Type

Paper — conceptual manuscript proposing a construct and research agenda. No new data, validated scale, prevalence estimate, or demonstrated effects of LLMorphism.

## Key Insight

Capraro proposes [[llmorphism]]: people may infer from similar linguistic outputs that human cognition works like a language model. The error is the inference from output resemblance to a general claim about underlying human thought, rather than every comparison between people and AI.

## Argument

Two proposed mechanisms are analogical transfer (projecting model properties onto humans) and metaphorical availability (using familiar AI vocabulary to explain thought). The paper distinguishes the idea from general machine metaphors, [[anthropomorphism-of-technology]], computational theories of mind, dehumanization, objectification, and embodied predictive-processing theories (pp. 2–5).

Potential consequences include treating people as interchangeable output producers, equating fluency with expertise, weakening responsibility, and neglecting embodied or contextual information. The author presents all these effects as hypotheses. Alternative metaphors, attention to differences between humans and models, and experience in relational work are proposed moderators, not tested safeguards (pp. 5–10).

## Key Passages

> "LLMorphism is the biased belief that human cognition works like a large language model."
> — Capraro, [p.1], Abstract

> "Importantly, LLMorphism does not consist in any comparison between humans and LLMs."
> — Capraro, [p.4]

> "there is not yet direct empirical evidence on its prevalence or effects."
> — Capraro, [p.5], Potential impacts

## Relevance

Names a distinct proposed risk to how people understand human thought and worth. It complements work on attributing human qualities to machines by considering the reverse direction.

## Supports

- [[llmorphism]] — source proposing the construct; support is conceptual.
- [[fluency-bias]] — hypothesizes a further route through which fluent output might be mistaken for understanding.
- [[embodied-cognition]] — embodied human experience is a central disanalogy in the argument.
- [[agency]] — proposes consequences for responsibility, without measuring them.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Extends: [[anthropomorphism-of-technology]] — reverse direction of attribution, not an interchangeable label.
- Does not establish that computational accounts of cognition are false or that all human–LLM comparisons are biased.

## Open Questions

- Is this a measurable construct distinct from existing machine metaphors and dehumanization?
- How common is it, and does it change behavior?
- Does greater technical knowledge reduce the proposed bias, or does exposure increase it?
