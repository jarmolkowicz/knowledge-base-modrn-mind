# When Expert Intuition Deserves Trust

## Citation

Kahneman, D., & Klein, G. (2009). Conditions for intuitive expertise: A failure to disagree. *American Psychologist*, 64(6), 515–526. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755).

## Type

Peer-reviewed theoretical synthesis reconciling heuristics-and-biases research with naturalistic decision making. No new pooled experiment. Solid status applies to the established expertise account, not to inferred effects of generative AI.

## Key Insight

Trust in intuition should depend on valid cues in the task environment and the person's opportunity to learn them through practice and feedback. Confidence alone cannot establish either condition.

## Evidence and Argument

The authors compare studies of expert performance, predictable bias, and human-versus-statistical forecasts. Skilled intuition is recognition of learned patterns; intuition can also arise from an inappropriate heuristic. Both can feel compelling. Environments need stable cue–outcome relationships, not certainty: poker can support skill despite uncertain individual outcomes (PDF pp.5–8).

Learning opportunities require repeated relevant experience and useful feedback. Delayed, ambiguous, or misleading feedback can support overconfidence instead. Expertise is often “fractionated”: competence in one task within a profession does not guarantee competence in another (pp.8–9).

Statistical decision aids may outperform people through consistent use of weak cues. Their use requires suitable variables, measurable outcomes, comparable cases, and monitoring for changed conditions. Supervision may itself weaken through [[automation-bias]]. A premortem is discussed as a way to draw on expertise while legitimizing dissent; its efficacy is not newly tested here (pp.9–10).

## Key Passages

> “Subjective experience is not a reliable indicator of judgment accuracy.”
> — Kahneman & Klein, [p.1], printed p.515

> “High validity does not imply the absence of uncertainty, and the regularities that are to be discovered are sometimes statistical.”
> — Kahneman & Klein, [p.6], printed p.520

> “There is no subjective marker that distinguishes correct intuitions from intuitions that are produced by highly imperfect heuristics.”
> — Kahneman & Klein, [p.8], printed p.522

## Relevance

Provides foundations for assessing human evaluators as well as AI outputs. [Inference] Someone reviewing an AI answer should ask whether they have learned this particular task with reliable outcome feedback, rather than treating a senior title or felt certainty as proof of evaluative skill.

## Supports

- [[judgment]] — identifies environmental and learning conditions for skilled intuition.
- [[calibration]] — checks should be specific to the task and feedback history.
- [[confidence-competence-gap]] — confidence can mislead without any AI involvement.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Complements [[ericsson-deliberate-practice-1993]] with the prior question of whether the environment supplies learnable valid cues. Practice duration alone is insufficient.
- Complements [[hatano-inagaki-adaptive-expertise-1986]]: familiarity with some problems does not guarantee reliable performance in unfamiliar ones.
- Qualifies any broad reading of [[paradox-of-expertise]]: neither trust nor distrust of expertise should be uniform across tasks.

## Limitations

Narrative synthesis, not a systematic meta-analysis conducted by the authors. Determining an environment's validity can itself be difficult. Historical examples concern the evidence available in 2009. The article does not test current LLMs or demonstrate AI-caused loss of skill; those applications remain inferences. Its discussion of statistical algorithms does not establish that a modern generative model has the same reliability or fairness.

## Open Questions

- Which workplace tasks supply timely, interpretable feedback today?
- [Inference] Can AI-assisted workflows preserve those learning opportunities while supporting useful decisions?
