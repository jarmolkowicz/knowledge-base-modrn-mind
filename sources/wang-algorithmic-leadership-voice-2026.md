# Wang et al. (2026) — Speaking Up to Algorithmic Leaders

## Citation

Wang, S., Sun, X., Ni, S., Wu, M., & Hu, K. (2026). Employees show greater willingness to voice toward algorithmic than human leaders in cognitive tasks through fairness perception and psychological safety. *Scientific Reports*. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-61031-6

## Type

Accepted, unedited manuscript reporting three scenario experiments. Page locators below refer to the supplied 86-page PDF.

## Key Insight

People can be more willing to challenge an algorithmic leader than a human leader when the problem is analytical and the leader invites input. These experiments measured stated willingness, not actual workplace speech or successful influence over decisions.

## Evidence

All three studies recruited Chinese participants through Credamo and randomized the depicted leader type.

- **Study 1, N = 186:** across workshop, driving, and marketing scenarios, willingness to speak up was higher for algorithmic leaders (reported means 7.13 versus 6.70 on a nine-point scale; partial η² = .038). PDF pp.23–31.
- **Study 2, N = 294:** the leader-type × task-type interaction was significant (partial η² = .023, p = .010). The advantage appeared for task-allocation problems, but not team-conflict problems. The original N = 198 sample was supplemented with 96 participants during revision to improve gender balance. PDF pp.31–38.
- **Study 3, N = 453:** an investment-team scenario produced higher perceived fairness, psychological safety, and voice intention for algorithmic leaders. The voice effect was small (partial η² = .012, p = .019). The standardized sequential indirect path through fairness and safety was −.047, 95% CI [−.090, −.018], with algorithmic leader coded 1 and human leader coded 2. Fairness alone was not a significant indirect path. PDF pp.39–47.

The mediation variables were measured in one session. Randomizing the leader does not establish the causal order of fairness, safety, and willingness.

## Key Passages

## Relevance

Adds a useful boundary to broad claims about AI reducing [[agency]]. A system perceived as less personally judgmental may make invited criticism feel safer. Perceived fairness is not a test of actual fairness, and willingness to complain is not proof that complaints change outcomes.

## Supports

- [[agency]] — evidence about one possible condition for expressing disagreement.
- [[affective-trust-deficit]] — the absent emotional-task advantage reinforces the importance of distinguishing task types.
- [[illusion-of-objectivity]] — [Inference] perceived algorithmic neutrality could encourage trust without proving neutrality; actual bias was not audited here.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Extends: [[alessandro-self-efficacy-2025]] — algorithmic evaluation and invited voice need not produce the same response. The different designs do not directly replicate or refute each other.
- Extends: [[georganta-team-trust-2024]] — moves from teammate trust to willingness to challenge a leader.

## Open Questions

- Does the effect persist when disagreement carries real consequences and the system has a record of ignoring complaints?
- Would human oversight, procedural appeals, or a less autonomous algorithm change responses?
- Samples came from one platform and national context; two were predominantly female.
- Some controls, including perceived leader ability, may themselves be affected by the manipulation. The adjusted comparisons should not be treated as unrestricted workplace causal effects.
