# Luettgau et al. (2026) — Personal Advice, Follow-Through and Well-Being

## Citation

Luettgau, L., Cheung, V., Dubois, M., Juechems, K., Bergs, J., Symes, L., Davidson, H., O’Dell, B., Kirk, H. R., Rollwage, M., & Summerfield, C. (2026). *People readily follow personal advice from AI but it does not improve their well-being*. arXiv:2511.15352v3, 17 April 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15352v3

## Type

Paper — longitudinal randomized trial; supplied version is a preprint.

## Key Insight

People frequently reported acting on AI advice after one conversation, including consequential advice. Yet discussing personal problems produced no sustained well-being advantage over discussing hobbies with the same chatbots. Following advice, feeling helped, and improved outcomes are separate measures.

## Evidence

- 6,474 UK adults; one 20-minute conversation; follow-up after 2–3 weeks with approximately 88% retention. Three models were studied in separate collection periods: GPT-4o in July 2025; Llama-3.3-70B and Gemini 3 Pro in February 2026 (PDF pp.3, 14–15).
- Eight advice conditions crossed safety-oriented prompting, actionability and access to personal information. The ninth condition was an active hobby/interest conversation control. There was no no-chat or human-advisor comparison (pp.3, 14).
- In advice conditions, 75–79% reported following advice, compared with 55.4% in the control. Advice-following remained above 60% at high stakes. Stakes were graded from conversation content rather than experimentally assigned; their relationship with adherence is correlational (pp.6–8).
- The pooled follow-up difference in a latent well-being score was approximately −.002, 95% highest posterior density interval [−.04, .03]. Personal-problem conversations produced lower immediate well-being than hobby conversations; that difference disappeared at follow-up. Measures of clinical threshold crossing or symptom deterioration also showed no reliable between-condition differences (pp.9–10).
- People who reported following advice also reported greater well-being improvement in both conditions. This does not establish that following advice caused the improvement (pp.10, 12).

## Key Passages

> "It is also noteworthy to consider that advice-following was self-reported rather than behaviourally identified, which may be subject to social desirability bias or inaccurate recall of one’s own behaviour."
> — Luettgau et al., PDF p.12

> "Thus, our findings should not be taken to imply that AI advice is generically safe, but merely that, in a common usage setting, we detected low levels of harm from a popular LLM with additional safeguards in place."
> — Luettgau et al., PDF p.12

## Relevance

Adds delayed, real-world self-reports to the evidence on AI influence. [Inference] A useful evaluation should check the consequences of advice, rather than treating adoption or immediate satisfaction as proof of value.

## Supports

- [[calibration]] — stakes reduced reported adherence only modestly; importance alone does not ensure careful reliance.
- [[metacognition]] — separates perceived advice value from later measured well-being.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Extends [[meincke-advice-quality-2026]]: adds reported follow-through and a later well-being outcome to work on advice evaluation; different tasks and comparisons prevent a direct effect-size comparison.
- Qualifies [[calibration]]: its general claim that high stakes reduce reliance needs this boundary condition; many participants still reported acting at high stakes.

## Limitations

An additional live safety classifier blocked and regenerated potentially harmful responses before users saw them. Its training labels had only slight-to-fair initial expert agreement (Cohen’s kappa .11–.35), followed by filtering/adjudication. Neither low flagged-harm rates nor similar deterioration rates establish the safety of ordinary unfiltered chatbot use (pp.5, 12, 15).

This was a single brief interaction with a general-population sample, largely nontechnical advice, and a short follow-up. It does not test crisis support, repeated dependence, clinical treatment, or benefits relative to no chatbot. Model comparisons are also complicated by different collection dates. Adherence alone is not evidence of uncritical acceptance or loss of decision control.

## Open Questions

- Do longer exposure, objective behavior measures, or no-chat and human-advisor controls change the result?
- Do people verify consequential advice before acting?
- The manuscript contains minor internal reporting inconsistencies: p.9 labels the follow-up contrast as Session 1 and a short-term personalization effect as Session 2; pp.27–29 call the stakes composite four-variable, while p.23 describes three. These should be resolved before using detailed secondary estimates.
