# Zhang et al. (2026) — AI Usage and Work Wellbeing

**Excluded by user, 2026-09-26.** Retained only for the audit trail; do not integrate or cite as an approved KB contribution.

## Citation

Zhang, C., Chen, S., Zhang, D., & Zhang, Q. (2026). The impact of artificial intelligence usage on affective work well-being: A self-determination theory perspective. *Behavioral Sciences*, 16, 670. https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16050670

## Type

Three-wave survey. **Draft requires evidence clarification before integration or use as positive counterevidence.** The issues below were checked against the rendered original PDF, not only extracted text.

## Key Insight

The authors propose that AI use supports wellbeing through meaningfulness and engagement, especially when collaboration is better. The supplied results contain unresolved reporting inconsistencies, so the paper cannot yet provide dependable quantitative support for that conclusion.

## Evidence Reported

The sample comprised 360 retained Chinese employees from 400 distributed questionnaires, recruited through Credamo. They worked in knowledge-intensive settings and regularly used AI. Surveys were separated by two weeks: AI use and the moderator at T1, meaningfulness and engagement at T2, wellbeing at T3.

Tables 4–6 report a positive total association (b = .124) and positive indirect paths through meaningfulness (.076, CI [.036, .115]) and engagement (.052, CI [.014, .096]). These are the authors' reported estimates, not independently validated effects. At low moderator values, the reported conditional indirect paths were non-significant.

## Reporting and Measurement Concerns

- Table 3 (PDF p.10) reports AI use–wellbeing r = −.178, meaningfulness–wellbeing r = .016, and engagement–wellbeing r = −.036. These do not straightforwardly match the positive narrative. Adjustment can change an association's sign, but the paper does not explain this pattern.
- Table 4 (PDF p.11) labels a predictor row “AWB” inside models whose outcome is also affective work wellbeing; the stated interaction term is not clearly labeled. The wellbeing-model intercepts also match Table 3's collaboration mean, not its wellbeing mean. Variable labeling or model specification needs clarification; no correction is assumed here.
- The reported sample items for collaboration “quality” ask whether AI participates in decisions, problem solving, and information evaluation. These examples measure involvement more directly than reliability, transparency, or supportiveness; the full questionnaire was not supplied.
- Basic psychological needs are used to explain the findings but were not directly measured. No experimental manipulation establishes an effect of AI adoption.
