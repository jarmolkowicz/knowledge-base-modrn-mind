# Screen all sources; closely read the promising ones

Date: 2026-09-26. Pre-screening of the inbox, not approval to integrate sources or practices.

## Purpose

Cover the whole collection without deeply reading every item. Select by extractable content and source quality, not by author fame, recency, or whether an article fits an outcome category we already expect.

The outcome taxonomy remains open. The screening question is: **Does this source contain a concrete way of working with AI, or a clearly relevant transferable practice, worth examining for this KB?**

## Questions for each source

1. **Fit:** Is there a plausible contribution to how people work, think, learn, create or exercise agency with AI? Record the contribution in the source's own terms. Faster output alone is not evidence of human capability growth, but do not assume a productivity-focused source contains nothing else.
2. **Specificity:** Is there an identifiable action, sequence, exercise, decision rule, or worked example? General encouragement, tool announcements and news commentary are usually background unless a useful practice is embedded within them.
3. **Basis:** Who originated or used it, in what context? Distinguish demonstrated use, first-person reporting, research, and an untested proposal. Relevant experience matters; a scholarly credential is not required. A plausible new exercise can remain a candidate without outcome data.
4. **Inspectability:** Can the relevant passage be located and read? Are authorship, original source and date clear? Treat a preview, summary or promotional claim as such.
5. **Incremental value:** Does it add a distinct practice, a useful variant, a worked example, a limit or counterexample? Repetition alone is neither novelty nor independent confirmation.

Record these judgments separately. Do not collapse them into an apparently precise numerical credibility score. A concrete practice in a promotional article can be worth assessing while its outcome claims are rejected.

## Screening decisions

| Decision | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Read for extraction | A plausible, sufficiently specific practice or valuable variant warrants closer reading |
| Read with flags | Potentially useful process, but claims, attribution, evidence or overlap need checking |
| Background / transfer / discovery | Useful context, a non-AI practice needing explicit adaptation, or a lead to another source |
| Hold for access or provenance | Missing relevant full text, uncertain authorship, conflicting copies, or extraction needed |
| Set aside for practice extraction | Clearly outside scope, no extractable practice after inspection, or a verified duplicate; retain the source and reason |
| Unclear | Insufficient inspection to decide; keep open |

These are recommendations for reading, not Stage 2 INCLUDE decisions or judgments that a practice works. Selected sources enter the same catalog → triage → distill → critique → integrate workflow as other sources. Reuse existing source workbenches where applicable. Do not duplicate an integrated source merely to extract practices from it.

## Reading depth

- **Short articles:** inspect the body, especially any process and example. Titles alone cannot justify exclusion.
- **Long articles:** headings, opening, conclusion, and candidate passages; expand reading when the decision is unclear. Record coverage.
- **Books:** screen at chapter/section level. A book with much background may still contain one valuable practice section. Keep the book as the source; record section locators.
- **Transcripts:** inspect the speaker and chapter/topic markers, then relevant timestamped passages with surrounding turns. Ignore sponsor pitches when identifying the speaker's practice. Do not reject an entire interview because its title is general.
- **Previews and show notes:** use for discovery and access planning; extract only what is actually described in accessible text, with explicit limits. Never reconstruct missing instructions.

## Prevent narrow selection

Screen all collections, older and newer material, before treating the shortlist as representative. Keep borderline items visible. Revisit a spread of set-aside items across authors and formats to look for missed practices; re-open a group if the exclusion rule was too narrow. Check which domains, practitioner backgrounds and types of human activity the shortlist omits.

The later taxonomy will emerge from close reading of selected material and explicit examination of gaps. Counts of keyword hits, article length, author output volume, repeated publication, and the existing risk labels must not determine it.
