# Basic Psychological Needs

## What It Is

Basic Psychological Need Theory distinguishes autonomy (acting with willingness and endorsement), competence (feeling effective) and relatedness (feeling genuinely connected). It treats their satisfaction as important for well-being, not merely as individual preferences.

## Why It Matters

An AI workflow can change these experiences in different directions. [Inference] Better output does not show that people feel ownership, effectiveness or connection—and feeling effective does not establish objective skill.

## Key Insight

Low satisfaction and active frustration are not interchangeable. Limited choice is not automatically experienced coercion; little connection is not identical to rejection. Chen et al. (2015) found better fit for separate satisfaction and frustration factors for each need, with different associations with well-being and depressive symptoms (PDF pp.11–16).

Their cross-sectional student studies support this distinction across the sampled countries, not a causal claim about all people or AI systems. Unique associations differed by outcome: competence satisfaction did not independently predict life satisfaction in Study 2, and relatedness had no unique well-being association in Study 1. These findings do not make either need irrelevant.

Autonomy means volition, not necessarily working independently. Competence here is experienced effectiveness, not tested ability or accurate calibration. Relatedness is connection, not simply interaction count. Need valuation, desire for more satisfaction, and actual reported satisfaction are also different measures.

Sheldon and Niemiec (2006) examined the profile of autonomy, felt competence and relatedness alongside their levels across four observational studies. More balanced profiles showed modest additional associations with adjustment. This was not uniform: in Study 1 the subjective well-being association was nonsignificant after squared need terms were included (PDF p.4). The findings do not establish that everyone needs equal scores, that improving one need harms people when others stay unchanged, or that deliberately equalizing scores improves well-being. Felt competence remains distinct from tested ability, and need ratings are not a measure of accountable control.

## Evidence limits

The supplied Chen proof has a Study 1 sample-count inconsistency (abstract 685; method groups sum to 683). Its first-study Chinese autonomy scales also had low reliability. Study 2 used 1,051 students split for scale development and cross-validation. The results do not establish worldwide universality, clinical diagnoses or workplace intervention effects. No score thresholds or questionnaire adaptation are proposed here.
