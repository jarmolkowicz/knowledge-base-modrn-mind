# Oppenheimer (2008) — The Secret Life of Fluency

## Citation

Oppenheimer, D. M. (2008). The secret life of fluency. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 12(6), 237–241.

**DOI:** [10.1016/j.tics.2008.02.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2008.02.014)

## Type

Paper (review)

## Key Insight

Oppenheimer reviews how processing ease can influence judgment indirectly: which cues people use, how concretely they represent a problem, and which reasoning strategy they adopt. The review also describes attribution effects: an obvious alternative explanation for ease can reduce or reverse its influence (pp.1–2).

These are context-sensitive findings from pre-generative-AI research. [Inference] They suggest questions about evaluating fluent AI output, but do not establish that AI always induces automatic acceptance or that making output harder to read improves judgment.

## Key Passages

> "Fluency – the subjective experience of ease or difficulty associated with completing a mental task – has been shown to be an influential cue in a wide array of judgments… recently researchers have begun to look at how fluency impacts judgment through more subtle and indirect routes."
> — Oppenheimer, [p.1] (abstract)

> "People judge fluent statements to be more true, more likeable, more frequent, more famous, better category members and to come from a more intelligent source than disfluent statements."
> — Oppenheimer, [p.1]

> "In the initial weeks after a company goes public, stocks with easier to pronounce names or ticker codes outperform stocks with more disfluent names or codes."
> — Oppenheimer, [p.1] (summarizing Alter & Oppenheimer)

> "When cues were written in a slightly degraded font, participants weighted the information less than when it was presented in an easier to process font."
> — Oppenheimer, [p.2]

> "Fluency is not a cognitive operation in and of itself but, rather, a feeling of ease associated with a cognitive operation… it can serve as a cue toward judgments in virtually any situation."
> — Oppenheimer, [p.1]

> "When there is an obvious alternative cause for fluency people will spontaneously discount the fluency experience, and the effects of fluency on judgment will be diminished or reversed."
> — Oppenheimer, [p.2] (Box 2)

## Relevance

Extends [[reber-schwarz-fluency-truth-1999]] beyond truth judgments to broader decision processes. Attribution discounting is a possible basis for interventions, not proof that an AI label will improve reasoning.

## Supports

- [[fluency-bias]] — review of fluency as a judgment cue.
- [[metacognition]] — subjective ease can inform monitoring and strategy choice.
- [[coherence-trap]] and [[automation-bias]] — [Inference] possible applications to AI; not studied here.
- [[think-first]] — [Inference] related practice, not evaluated by the review.

## Contradicts / Extends

Reviews a broader range of fluency effects than [[reber-schwarz-fluency-truth-1999]]. Dual-process accounts are part of the explanation, not a universal rule that ease prevents deliberation.

## Open Questions

- How do these effects transfer from short stimuli to long AI-generated arguments?
- Which visual and linguistic features affect judgment independently of content?
- Does source disclosure improve accuracy, merely change trust, or have different effects across tasks? [[cheong-penalizing-transparency-2025]] and [[reimann-schilke-disclosure-2025]] concern related but distinct disclosure outcomes.
