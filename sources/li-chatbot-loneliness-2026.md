# Human Peer Contact, Chatbot Support, and Loneliness

## Citation

Li, R.-N., Folk, D., Singh, A., Ungar, L., & Dunn, E. (2026). Is a random human peer better than a highly supportive chatbot in reducing loneliness over time? *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 125, 104911. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2026.104911).

## Type

Peer-reviewed, preregistered two-week randomized study. Emerging status reflects the narrow population, single chatbot, and short duration.

## Key Insight

Daily contact with a supportive chatbot reduced negative affect in exploratory analyses but did not reduce loneliness relative to journaling. A randomly assigned human peer produced lower post-study loneliness than both alternatives.

## Methods and Results

First-semester students at a Canadian university used private Discord rooms for 14 days: texting another student, texting a GPT-4o-mini chatbot named Sam, or writing a one-sentence daily journal. Sam used memory and supportive instructions. Human pairs briefly met at setup. Participants sent about 9–10 messages daily in the conversational groups (pp.2–3).

Of 306 assigned students, 296 contributed to daily analyses, 276 to pre/post measures, and 275 to final baseline-adjusted analyses. Reported condition counts—65 control, 75 AI, 135 human—sum to 275, although the participant paragraph places them immediately after the 296 figure. Keep these denominators distinct.

Baseline-adjusted UCLA loneliness means were 1.85 for human peers, 1.98 for AI, and 2.00 for control. Human versus AI: b=-0.13, p=.011; human versus control: b=-0.14, p=.007; AI versus control: p=.821. No significant condition effect appeared on daily recalled social connection (pp.3–4).

Exploratory analyses found lower negative mood in both conversational groups than control, while positive mood benefits favored humans. Continued use after the assigned period was 33% with human peers, 14% with Sam, and 3% for journaling (N=231 in this analysis). GPT-coded conversation analysis rated Sam as more empathic, yet participants expressed more empathy toward humans. Proposed reciprocal-support mechanisms were not tested with a powered mediation design (pp.4–6).

## Relevance

An active journaling control helps distinguish the effect of receiving chatbot responses from writing about one's life. Mood, recalled connection, loneliness across weeks, and continued use are different outcomes; none should stand in for all the others.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Extends [[fang-ai-loneliness-2025]] with an active non-AI control, which helps address regression-to-the-mean and repeated-measure explanations.
- Complements [[folk-dunn-companionship-loneliness-2026]]; this short experiment found no chatbot advantage over journaling, not a demonstrated increase in loneliness caused by chatbot use.
- Qualifies extrapolation from [[folk-heine-dunn-anthropomorphism-2025]]: immediate connection and two-week loneliness are not interchangeable endpoints.

## Limitations

Mostly female first-year students, generally mild baseline loneliness, one text chatbot and two weeks. Human peers had initial face-to-face contact. Daily mood and connection ratings were retrospective. Table 1 standardized-effect estimates remain uncertain because the available text has malformed minus signs and apparently inconsistent confidence intervals. Model-rated empathy is not equivalent to a demonstrated mechanism. No inference about clinical treatment effectiveness follows from this study. Hosted supplements were not included in the supplied PDF.

## Open Questions

- Would an entirely online human pairing show the same advantage?
- Could AI designed to support contact with other people reduce loneliness more than a surrogate companion?
