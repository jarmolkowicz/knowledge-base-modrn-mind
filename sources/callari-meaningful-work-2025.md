# Callari & Puppione (2025) — Meaningful Work and Copilot Practices

## Citation

Callari, T. C., & Puppione, L. (2025). Meaningful work as shaped by employee work practices in human-AI collaborative environments: a qualitative exploration through ideal types. *European Journal of Innovation Management, 28*(10), 5001–5027. https://doi.org/10.1108/EJIM-11-2024-1339

## Type

Paper — qualitative analysis of open-ended survey responses during a workplace Microsoft 365 Copilot trial.

## Key Insight

Employees can find meaning in quite different ways of using AI: delegating routine tasks while retaining control, refining an assistant's suggestions, or using AI to challenge their thinking. More intensive adoption is not demonstrated to be a better path to meaningful work.

## Evidence

The researchers surveyed 802 licensed users in one multinational company during July–August 2024 and received 357 responses. This paper analyzes the open-ended answers, not causal effects of trial assignment or the survey's quantitative scales (pp. 5006–5009; [p.6–9]).

The authors combined thematic coding, within- and cross-case comparisons, case summaries, and selected representative cases into three ideal types. The lead researcher recoded after 14 days to check stability. Ideal types are interpretive abstractions, not validated personality classes, measured competence levels, or established stages of progress.

- **Efficiency-Seeking:** delegates routine work, favors low prompting effort, and protects independent decisions.
- **Pragmatic Integrator:** uses AI for ideas and initial information, checks and refines outputs, and worries about reduced colleague interaction.
- **Collaborative Optimiser:** seeks an expert-like sparring partner, invests effort in prompting, and still claims final authority. Some descriptions are aspirations for future capabilities; one participant explicitly asks for less agreement and more challenge (pp. 5010–5016).

The discussion interprets meaning as shaped jointly by task discretion, organizational arrangements, perceived expertise, and recognition from others. It also warns that felt control can coexist with system constraints. These are interpretations of reported experience, not measured changes in independent capability or decision authority.

## Relevance

Supplies concrete workplace accounts of retained [[agency]] and [[professional-identity-threat]], including positive and cautious interpretations of the same tool. Training can respond to the role people want AI to play rather than assuming everyone should progress toward maximum delegation.

## Supports

- [[ai-work-practice-ideal-types]] — three descriptive orientations for discussing task choices and support needs.
- [[agency]] — employees actively define what to delegate and what authority to keep.
- [[professional-identity-threat]] — concerns about expertise coexist with perceived benefits.
- [[metacognitive-demand]] — evaluating, revising, and deciding how to prompt require effort.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Extends: [[identity-threat-coping]] — adoption orientations are distinct from reactions to threatened psychological needs.
- Qualifies a uniform loss-of-meaning account: some employees describe greater meaning from bounded automation or a challenging exchange, but this study does not establish lasting benefits.

## Open Questions

- One company, voluntary responses, brief open-text data, and early adoption limit transfer. Nonrespondents may differ.
- No longitudinal evidence shows people moving between types or retaining skills.
- Theme counts in Table 3 are coded material, not employee prevalence; do not convert them into percentages of users.
- Table 1 contains a visible gender count/percentage inconsistency. This draft avoids those demographics.
- User descriptions of AI capability and learning are perceptions, not technical evaluations.
