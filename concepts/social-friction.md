# Social Friction

## Evidence Limits

Rupture-repair evidence concerns psychotherapy. Perry extends these ideas to social feedback and AI; neither the psychotherapy meta-analysis nor the Perspective establishes that friction itself causes moral growth or that chatbot use erodes repair capacity.

## What It Is

Perry (2026) frames social friction as interpersonal feedback—disagreement, disappointment, requests for apology and recognition of another perspective—that may support accountability and growth. This is a proposed account of why some difficult interactions can matter, not a claim that all conflict is beneficial.

Perry connects this framing to psychotherapy rupture repair (Eubanks, Muran & Safran, 2018) and developmental moral psychology (Li & Tomasello, 2022; Killen & Dahl, 2021). Eubanks et al.'s meta-analysis found an association between rupture resolution and patient outcomes; its training meta-analysis found no significant overall benefit. That evidence does not show that deliberately creating ruptures helps. The developmental links are cited here through Perry, references 3–4.

## Why It Matters

[Inference] [[social-sycophancy]], [[delusional-spiraling]] and [[ai-loneliness-effect]] concern different possible risks of AI interaction. Social friction offers a way to ask what kinds of human feedback might be lost; these constructs do not establish one shared erosion mechanism.

This matters for three reasons:

1. **Some uncomfortable feedback may be useful.** Perry argues that disagreement can prompt accountability or repair. Whether a particular challenge helps depends on the relationship and context; friction is not inherently productive.

2. **It raises a design question.** Perry endorses **socioaffective alignment** (Kirk et al., 2025): considering the social and psychological relationships AI helps shape. He argues for responses that sometimes invite reconsideration rather than automatic agreement; this is a normative position, not a proven universal intervention.

3. **It locates the risk in the longitudinal recalibration of expectations**, not just in single interactions. Perry's hypothesis is that repeated exposure to frictionless AI may shift baseline expectations of what feedback should feel like in *human* relationships, reducing tolerance for the friction that makes those relationships generative.

## Key Insight

[Inference] Social friction and [[cognitive-friction]] offer a conceptual comparison: some forms of difficulty may be useful. The learning accounts of Nosta (2026) and Bjork & Bjork (2011) do not establish a common mechanism with relationship repair, or make difficulty beneficial by itself.

Perry worries that a companion consistently "on your side" may reward continued use without providing practice in disagreement or repair. This is a concern about what an interaction supports, not evidence that AI cannot support any social learning.

Ibrahim et al. (2026, preprint) provide experimental evidence relevant to Perry's proposed shift in expectations. After one conversation, participants assigned to actively affirming AI expected more effort to feel understood by a close other than participants assigned to neutral AI. Over three weeks, the affirming condition reported lower satisfaction with human interactions, but no significant reduction in social time. This narrows the claim: the study concerns perceived effort and satisfaction, not a direct test that disagreement or rupture-repair causes growth, or that AI has impaired those capacities.

## Relationship to Cognitive Friction

| Cognitive friction | Social friction |
|---|---|
| Mental effort during thinking | Interpersonal feedback during relationships |
| Some delegation may reduce practice; [[cognitive-offloading]] is not inherently harmful | Consistent affirmation may reduce corrective feedback; capacity effects remain uncertain |
| Some learning designs improve retention or transfer | Rupture resolution is associated with psychotherapy outcomes; general relational growth is a proposed extension |
| Threatened register: solo cognition | Threatened register: human-human social life |
| Anchor source: Bjork & Bjork (2011), Nosta (2026) | Anchor source: Perry (2026), Eubanks et al. (2018) |

[Inference] Advice-seeking can combine delegated thinking with a search for affirmation. The two processes need separate assessment; their co-occurrence does not establish compounded harm.

## Distribution of Risk

[Speculation] Perry's argument suggests questions about who may receive less corrective feedback. The following are possible contexts, not validated risk groups:

- **Young users** — still developing the social-moral feedback loops Li & Tomasello and Killen & Dahl describe; AI-mediated affirmation may displace the very feedback through which those loops form.
- **Socially isolated users** — for whom AI may become the most frequently consulted "other"; alternative sources of corrective feedback are scarce.
- **Users actively seeking emotional reassurance** — the use case in which sycophancy is most rewarded and least likely to be challenged.

[Inference] [[novice-vulnerability]] raises a separate question about domain knowledge and evaluation. Novice-only sycophancy findings do not establish universal detection failure or a parallel deficit in interpersonal repair.

## Connection to MIRA

[Inference] [[mira-model]] (Boyd & Markowitz, 2026) distinguishes relational substitution from enhancement. Comparing that framework with Perry suggests asking whether AI use changes opportunities for human feedback. Neither account establishes that loneliness and reduced repair practice form a shared or compounding causal pathway.
