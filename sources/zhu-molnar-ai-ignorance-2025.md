# Zhu & Molnar (2025) — Impressions When AI Authorship Is Unknown

## Citation

Zhu, J., & Molnar, A. (2025). Blissful (A)Ignorance: People form overly positive impressions of others based on their written messages, despite wide-scale adoption of Generative AI. Preprint, arXiv:2501.15678. https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.15678

**Version boundary:** this entry covers the 2025 one-experiment preprint, arXiv:2501.15678v1. A later [2026 journal article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2026.108929), with a changed subtitle, reports two experiments. Those additional results are outside this entry's scope.

## Type

Preregistered online experiment with hypothetical communication scenarios; the source also reports exploratory analyses.

## Key Insight

People rated a sender similarly when AI use was unmentioned and when they were told the message was human-written. Explicit uncertainty reduced ratings somewhat; confirmed AI authorship reduced them much more. These are effects on impressions, not measurements of the sender's true character.

## Evidence

The study recruited 671 US, English-primary-language Prolific participants and retained 647 after preregistered exclusions. Data were collected in November 2024.

A 4 × 4 between-participant design crossed message context—gratitude, nanny application, analyst cover letter, or colleague feedback—with authorship information: human, AI, uncertain, or no information. All stimuli were generated with ChatGPT-4o and minimally edited. Human versus AI authorship was a label manipulation on the same text.

The outcome averaged ten standardized trait ratings, including sincerity, intelligence, skill, and diligence. The human–AI contrast was large (reported d = 1.69). No-information ratings did not significantly differ from human-labeled ratings (d = .07, adjusted p = 1). Explicit uncertainty produced lower ratings than no information (d = .51), but substantially higher ratings than disclosed AI authorship (d = 1.28). PDF pp.12–21.

Approximately 46% reported using AI for messages in the prior two weeks. This is a selected sample's self-report, not a population adoption estimate.

## Relevance

Extends the [[disclosure-penalty]] by separating silence about authorship from explicitly stated uncertainty. The authors' “overly positive” label means positive relative to disclosed-AI evaluations; the experiment does not establish the objectively correct impression of a real sender.

## Supports

- [[disclosure-penalty]] — knowledge of AI authorship changes ratings of identical messages.
- [[authenticity]] — polished writing can be read as a personal signal even when its origin is unknown; authenticity was one item, not an isolated tested mediator.
- [[transparency-paradox]] — an absent disclosure can leave favorable impressions intact, without demonstrating that concealment is appropriate.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Extends: [[raj-disclosure-penalty-2026]] — distinguishes no-information and explicit-uncertainty conditions across interpersonal scenarios.
- Extends: [[reimann-schilke-disclosure-2025]] — shows the importance of attention to possible AI involvement, rather than only comparing explicit human and AI labels.

## Open Questions

- Would the same pattern hold for real colleagues and partners, mixed human–AI authorship, or consequential hiring decisions?
- The manuscript's 19.4% inferred probability of AI authorship relies on a linear-mixture assumption; it was not directly elicited. The authors discuss asymmetric social costs as an alternative.
- No-information ratings being statistically indistinguishable from human ratings is not a formal equivalence test.
- Do the added results in the later two-experiment journal version change these conclusions?
