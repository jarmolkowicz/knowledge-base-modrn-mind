# Cash et al. (2026) — Specific Skills Versus Basic Cognitive Abilities

## Citation

Cash, T. N., Kelly, M. O., Macnamara, B. N., & Risko, E. F. (2026). Is AI making us stupid? Trends in Cognitive Sciences. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2026.06.004

Supplied three-page journal pre-proof with volume/issue placeholders. Locators refer to this file. Citation and DOI printed in the paper.

Audit: [ingestion record](../raw/cash-ai-stupid-2026/log.md).

## Type

Science & Society commentary; selective synthesis and argument, not a new experiment or meta-analysis.

## Key Insight

Evidence that offloading limits practice of a particular skill does not establish a decline in basic cognitive abilities. Consequences depend on what users delegate and how they engage with AI.

## Key Findings

- Distinguishes learned skills, acquired knowledge and basic abilities such as working memory. Missed acquisition differs from decay of an existing skill (p.1).
- The argument for greater resilience of basic abilities draws partly on limited general transfer of cognitive training. This is indirect reasoning, not a longitudinal AI result (pp.1–2).
- Box 1 separates information available from a tool from knowledge retained in the person's memory (p.2).
- Guided tutoring, feedback and learning from examples may have different consequences from answer substitution (p.2).
- Long-term effects, developmental differences and protective schedules remain unresolved (p.3).

## Relevance

Qualifies [[capacity-erosion]] and [[cognitive-offloading]]. Do not collapse failure to learn, loss of a practiced skill and change in general cognitive abilities.

## Supports

- [[capacity-erosion]] — distinguish the outcomes before assessing evidence.
- [[cognitive-offloading]] — consequences depend on activity and mode of use.
- [[partial-automation-principle]] — meaningful engagement is a conditional consideration, not a universal rule.

## Contradicts / Extends

Extends [[bastani-guardrails-math-rct-2025]] as a synthesis, not an independent replication. Qualifies claims that AI necessarily weakens general cognition without proving that basic abilities are unaffected.

## Limitations

Brief selective commentary, no systematic search or new data. Some cited skill-loss evidence is observational; do not upgrade it to causal or long-term proof.

## Open Questions

- Which skills are lost, retained or newly developed under specific uses?
- Do effects accumulate across years or differ by developmental stage?
