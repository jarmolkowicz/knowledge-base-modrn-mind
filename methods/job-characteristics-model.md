# Job Characteristics Model

## Overview

Hackman and Oldham's (1976) descriptive model connects features of an individual's job with experienced meaning, responsibility and knowledge of results. It offers a vocabulary for examining work design, not a proven AI-deployment recipe.

## What It Is / How It Works

The proposed mappings are:

| Job feature | Proposed psychological state |
|---|---|
| Skill variety, a whole identifiable task, and significance to others | Experienced meaningfulness |
| Discretion over scheduling and procedures | Experienced responsibility |
| Clear feedback from doing the work | Knowledge of results |

The model links these states with internal motivation and other work outcomes, with growth-need strength as a proposed moderator. Job-design autonomy here concerns discretion; it should not be silently equated with volitional autonomy in Basic Psychological Need Theory.

## What To Do

[Inference] For an AI-related job redesign, use the dimensions as discussion prompts: what skills remain in use, can the worker see a complete contribution, whose work or life benefits, what decisions remain theirs, and what reveals the quality of the result? Compare the actual before-and-after job. These questions adapt the model to AI-related work; the paper does not validate this intervention.

## Why It Works

The 1976 study tested associations among 658 employees in 62 jobs across seven organizations. Findings broadly supported parts of the model, but autonomy did not connect to responsibility only in the predicted way, and performance/absence relationships were weaker than self-reported attitude relationships. The design was observational, not a job-redesign experiment (PDF pp.10–28).

## Strengths / Limitations

- Separates job features from psychological experiences and outcomes.
- The model's multiplicative Motivating Potential Score was not shown superior to simpler alternatives; do not use it as an AI-safety or meaning score.
- The paper explicitly leaves practical change-project validation open. It focuses on largely independent individual jobs, not team-task design.
- Growth-need moderation does not show that people with lower growth scores should receive less autonomy or meaningful work.

## When It Applies

Background for comparing how a person's job changes. Insufficient on its own for team design, rights, workload, pay, power or long-term capability assessment.
