# AI Effects on Self-Efficacy, Self-Objectification, and Free Will Beliefs

## Citation

Gabbiadini, A., Durante, F., Baldissarri, C., Manfredi, A., Sterlicchio, A., & Romano, S. (2025). Artificial intelligence in the workplace: Effects on self-efficacy, self-objectification and beliefs in free will. *Journal of Community & Applied Social Psychology*, 35, e70107. https://doi.org/10.1002/casp.70107

## Type

Paper

## Key Insight

In these recruiting experiments, AI evaluation (vs. human evaluation) lowered reported self-efficacy and increased self-objectification. The fitted indirect path linked these differences to lower beliefs in free will. The effect occurs even in brief, scenario-based interactions and replicates with a real AI recruiting system. The fitted serial mediation is consistent with that sequence; the mediators were not independently randomized.

## Relevance

Provides experimental evidence of self-objectification in AI recruiting contexts. Being evaluated by AI differs from feeling like an editor rather than a creator when using it. [Inference] Similar effects could matter for other workplace evaluations, but performance reviews and monitoring were not tested.

## Key Findings

- **Consistent across 3 experiments** (~571 participants): AI recruiter condition vs. human recruiter
- **Self-efficacy**: Significantly lower when evaluated by AI vs. human
- **Self-objectification**: Significantly higher when evaluated by AI — participants attributed fewer mental states to themselves
- **Free will beliefs**: Lower beliefs were linked to self-objectification in the fitted mediation model
- **Fitted mediation sequence**: AI interaction --> self-efficacy reduction --> self-objectification --> reduced free will beliefs
- **Ecological validity**: Study 3 used a real AI-based recruiting system (not just scenarios)
- **Theoretical grounding**: Builds on Baldissarri et al.'s work objectification framework and Bandura's self-efficacy theory
