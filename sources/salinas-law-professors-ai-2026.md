# Salinas et al. (2026)

## Citation

Salinas, A., et al. (2026). Law professors prefer AI over peer answers. Working paper, May 27.

Supplied 61-page manuscript dated May 27, 2026. Author-hosted copy: https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/salinas_et_al.pdf. No journal peer-review status assumed.

[Retained source text](../raw/salinas-law-professors-ai-2026/source.md).

## Type

Blinded paired-answer evaluation by 16 U.S. contracts professors from 14 law schools, with a separate LLM-judge extension.

## Key Insight

Experts can prefer AI answers even for questions requiring disciplinary judgment. That measures the quality of an answer, not whether a student learns judgment from it.

## Key Findings

- Professors evaluated 40 questions through 2,918 anonymized comparisons. Two model systems had an average win rate of 75.33% against instructor answers (abstract; pp.5–7).
- Model answers received fewer expert harmfulness flags, but these were judgments about likely interference with learning—not observed student harm or learning outcomes (pp.8,19).
- Measured textual features did not fully explain model preference. Their associations were not causal, and this does not exclude all unmeasured style effects (pp.9–10).
- Broader model rankings came from an LLM judge validated against human preferences, not direct expert ratings of every model (pp.10–11,21).
- Authors explicitly separate answer quality from learning impact and richer tutoring interactions (p.14).

## Relevance

Counterevidence to a blanket claim that AI cannot produce high-quality answers when rules leave room for judgment.

## Supports

- [[judgment]] — distinguish evaluated output, human capacity and responsibility.

## Contradicts / Extends

Qualifies the contrast between rule-following AI and human judgment. It does not establish human-like internal reasoning or transfer of judgment to students.

## Limitations

- Small, self-selected instructor sample, one subject and shared casebook; 16 of 60 invited instructors participated.
- Instructors wrote brief answers under a roughly three-minute target without additional research; model conditions were not identical to professional practice.
- Forced-choice preferences allowed no ties; relative preference is not an absolute correctness guarantee.
- No student learning, retention, unaided transfer or real-world legal performance was measured.
- LLM-judge rankings carry additional position, verbosity and model-family bias risks.

## Open Questions

- Do preferred answers improve students' unaided reasoning and transfer?
- Does the result extend to interactive tutoring, other subjects and other instructor populations?
