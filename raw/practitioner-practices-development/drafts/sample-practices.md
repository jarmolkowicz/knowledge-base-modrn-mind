# Four sample practice cards

Date: 2026-09-26  
State: accepted format illustrations, not integrated practices or an approved ingestion batch. Fresh extraction will follow the source refresh. Titles below are editorial descriptions. Steps paraphrase the cited accounts; additions are marked. None of these accounts establishes lasting improvement in unaided capability.

## 1. Get feedback without handing over the rewrite

**Use when:** improving a piece of writing while keeping responsibility for its language and intent.

**Origin:** Rebecca J. Hogue, guest contribution to Slow AI, “How Can Writers Use AI Ethically?” (2025-12-06), sections “Step-by-step” and “A moment from Rebecca J Hogue.” [Original](https://theslowai.substack.com/p/how-can-writers-use-ai-ethically) · [Local copy](../../inbox/Practical/Sam%20Illingworth/how-can-writers-use-ai-ethically.md).

**Try it:** choose a short piece of your writing; ask AI to identify five areas for improvement without rewriting; judge the feedback against your intent; make the edits yourself. Hogue's source suggests a sample of up to 200 words. These quantities are exercise choices, not validated thresholds.

**Evidence:** first-person use with rough and polished text, example feedback, and explicit disagreement with some suggestions. Hogue reports finding it useful for learning; there is no independent learning measure.

**Limits:** feedback can be generic or flatten intentional style. An AI critique is another opinion. [Inference] Add a brief note explaining one suggestion you accepted and one you rejected. This records judgment; it does not measure improved judgment.

**Fit:** candidate variant of [[think-first]] or [[socratic-partnership]], with a specific no-rewrite boundary. Compare before creating a new method.

## 2. Test AI on the work you actually need

**Use when:** deciding where and how to rely on an AI system.

**Origin:** Ethan Mollick, “Giving your AI a Job Interview” (2025-11-12), section “Interview your model.” [Original](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/giving-your-ai-a-job-interview) · [Local copy](../../inbox/Practical/Ethan%20Mollick/giving-your-ai-a-job-interview.md).

**Try it:** construct realistic tasks and judgment scenarios; run them repeatedly; have people with relevant expertise assess the results; compare systems; repeat when systems change. Keep a record of where the system succeeds and fails.

**Evidence:** practitioner recommendation illustrated by Mollick's repeated business-idea ratings and discussion of external evaluation research. This is not a direct trial of whether adopting the routine improves users' judgment.

**Limits:** attractive examples may not represent daily work. [Inference] Define your criteria before reviewing outputs and include difficult cases. Keep factual accuracy separate from risk preference. Those safeguards are proposed additions here.

**Fit:** likely an addition to [[calibration]], connected to [[jagged-frontier]]. New source, not automatically a new method.

## 3. Practise reasoning through realistic work scenarios

**Use when:** someone wants more opportunities to practise a particular reasoning skill between real assignments.

**Origin:** Hilary Gridley on Lenny's Podcast, “How to build a team that can take a punch” (2025-06-15), 01:26:46–01:28:29. [Recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xm5QAzAlqEY&t=5206s) · [Local transcript](../../inbox/Practical/Lenny%27s%20Podcast/repo/episodes/hilary-gridley/transcript.md).

**Try it:** generate a logical-reasoning question set in a familiar product situation; choose among the options; inspect the explanation of why the answer is right or wrong. Gridley describes an “Aristotle” GPT using this process.

**Evidence:** a named practitioner's account of a tool she built, with a concrete scenario. The inspected passage offers no measurement of retention or transfer to actual product decisions.

**Limits:** a generated question or answer key can be wrong. A tidy reasoning puzzle is narrower than real judgment. [Inference] Have a knowledgeable person check sample questions and explanations; later try a fresh scenario without AI feedback. Preserve real colleague feedback as well.

**Fit:** possible new method for targeted reasoning practice. [[socratic-partnership]] concerns challenging one's existing position; this practice supplies new situations to attempt. Related concept: [[judgment]].

## 4. Turn creative discussions into your own working principles

**Use when:** repeated AI feedback reveals patterns in your creative choices that you want to articulate and use independently.

**Origin:** songwriter Joshua Sherman, contributor to “How Creatives Are Actually Using AI (And What They Refuse to Let It Do),” Slow AI (2026-03-17), contributor section “Joshua Sherman.” The article credits Alyssa Fu Ward as co-editor. [Original](https://theslowai.substack.com/p/how-creatives-actually-use-ai) · [Local copy](../../inbox/Practical/Sam%20Illingworth/how-creatives-actually-use-ai.md).

**Try it:** bring a work in progress to AI; debate feedback rather than automatically applying it; use the discussion to identify recurring principles; keep the principles you endorse and apply them in later work without AI. Sherman describes doing this with songwriting and a personal checklist.

**Evidence:** a first-person account with two song examples and a stated independent use of the checklist. No comparison, retention measure, or external quality assessment is provided.

**Limits:** a checklist can freeze current taste or repeat the model's preferences. [Inference] Keep an example and an exception beside each principle, and revise principles when the work calls for it. This is an editorial extension.

**Fit:** a possible extension to [[socratic-partnership]], emphasizing what the person carries into later work. Links to [[metacognition]] and [[authenticity]] are conceptual connections, not proof of benefit.
