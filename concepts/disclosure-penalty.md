# Disclosure Penalty

## What It Is

A disclosure penalty is a lower evaluation associated with revealing or labeling AI involvement in the settings studied. The outcome may be trust, perceived competence or a work rating. An AI-label effect on identical content is distinct from judging how much work was delegated or whether use was acknowledged; these comparisons need not produce the same pattern.

## Why It Matters

Disclosure can carry social costs in studied settings. Existing evidence covers evaluations of creative work, professional scenarios, messages and advice. These findings do not establish a universal penalty on every trait, nor do evaluation ratings alone demonstrate actual career or relationship losses.

## Key Insight

Schilke and Reimann (2025), as summarized in [[reimann-schilke-disclosure-2025]], report trust reductions across 13 experiments spanning several tasks and actor roles, including tests of softer disclosure wording. Those results concern the tested settings and disclosures; they do not establish that all assistance or all forms of acknowledgment reduce trust.

Raj et al. (2026) report a large creative-writing evidence base: 16 preregistered experiments (N = 27,491) showing a consistent 6.2% average reduction in evaluations of creative writing when AI involvement is disclosed (Cohen's d = 0.24). Perceived authenticity statistically mediated evaluations in their analyses — identical content is judged as less authentic when labeled as AI-generated. Critically, multiple evidence-based mitigation strategies (human-AI collaboration framing, AI humanization, capability priming, human-in-the-loop) all failed to reliably reduce the penalty. The effect was stable across 15 months (March 2023 to June 2024) despite growing public familiarity with generative AI.

Meincke, Nave and Terwiesch (2026) examine source disclosure in ethical advice. In a Registered Report (N=642 main study), AI ethical advice was preferred over expert advice in 46.8% of choices when the source was disclosed, but rose to 53.7% when source labels were hidden — a ~7 percentage-point penalty for the AI label, holding the actual advice content constant. In a parallel rating analysis (N=4,230 ratings, 423 participants), the source-by-disclosure interaction was significant (B=-0.39, d=-0.25, p<.001): when source was disclosed, expert advice was rated higher than AI (d=0.18, p<.001); when source was hidden, AI was rated on par with or slightly better than the expert (point estimate reversed, p=.082, n.s.). Same population, same advice content, same dilemmas — only the source label changed. This extends the evidence to ethical-advice evaluations; transparency norms themselves were not ranked or experimentally compared.

Zhu and Molnar's (2025) preregistered experiment (N = 647) distinguishes an undisclosed origin from explicit uncertainty. Identical AI-generated messages elicited similar sender impressions when labeled human-written or when authorship was unmentioned; explicit uncertainty reduced evaluations, while disclosed AI authorship produced the lowest ratings. The no-information versus uncertain contrast was d = .51. This adds an attention boundary to the [[disclosure-penalty]]: whether the possibility of AI use is raised matters, even without resolving authorship. These were hypothetical scenarios, and the absence of a significant human/no-information difference is not proof of equivalence.

Claessens et al. (2026) distinguish assistance with human editing from copying AI output, and acknowledgment from non-acknowledgment. In Study 2, honest assistance had higher pooled morality and trust ratings than the unaided control, alongside greater perceived laziness. Some relational tasks still attracted negative judgments under honest assistance. Full outsourcing generally compared less favorably with assistance. These are task- and trait-sensitive observer judgments, not established career consequences.

## Acknowledgment and delegation

The existing Schilke and Reimann (2025) source reports greater trust loss when AI use was exposed by a third party than when disclosed voluntarily, in their tested settings. Claessens et al. (2026) separately found that observers rated non-acknowledgment less favorably on morality and trust than acknowledgment. Neither comparison measures the real-world probability of discovering concealed use. These findings must not be combined with AI-label effects into advice to hide AI involvement. The extent of delegation and the honesty of the description are separate questions.
