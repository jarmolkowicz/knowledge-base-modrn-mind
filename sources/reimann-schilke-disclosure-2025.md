# Schilke & Reimann (2025) — The Transparency Dilemma

## Citation

Schilke, O., & Reimann, M. (2025). The transparency dilemma: How AI disclosure erodes trust. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 188, 104405.

**DOI:** [10.1016/j.obhdp.2025.104405](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2025.104405)

## Type

Paper (13-experiment empirical bundle, 5,000+ participants)

## Key Insight

Across **13 pre-registered experiments** spanning tasks (writing, analytics, art, hiring, financial services), actor types (supervisors, subordinates, professors, analysts, creatives, investment funds), and disclosure conditions, **actors who disclose their AI usage are trusted less than those who do not**. The trust penalty:

- holds **across diverse tasks** and actor roles,
- holds **above and beyond algorithm aversion** (it isn't just discomfort with AI itself),
- holds whether disclosure is **voluntary or mandatory**,
- holds **across different disclosure framings**, including soft language ("only used for grammar checking", "human-reviewed"),
- is **larger when undisclosed AI use is exposed by a third party** than when disclosed voluntarily — i.e., getting caught is worse than transparency, but transparency still costs trust.

The proposed mechanism is **legitimacy**, drawing on micro-institutional theory: AI disclosure signals deviation from the normative expectation that work outputs reflect "human expertise, judgment, and reasoning." Studies 6–8 support this account using measured mediation, a manipulation of collective validity and a causal-chain design. These tests strengthen the explanation without establishing legitimacy as the only possible mechanism.

A within-paper meta-analysis shows the trust penalty is **attenuated but not eliminated** by favorable technology attitudes and high perceived AI accuracy — and crucially, was **not attenuated** among evaluators who themselves use AI or are AI-familiar. This does not establish how the penalty changes over time as AI diffuses.

The findings inform [[disclosure-penalty]] and [[transparency-paradox]]: voluntary disclosure reduced trust in the studied scenarios, while third-party exposure of undisclosed use had a larger cost. Trust ratings alone do not determine disclosure obligations or all consequences of disclosure.

## Relevance

Tests disclosure across multiple tasks and roles, and offers evidence for perceived legitimacy as one explanatory process. Familiarity and prior AI use did not significantly attenuate the penalty in the within-paper meta-analysis. This is a between-person moderator result, not a longitudinal demonstration that the penalty persists as social norms change.

## Contradicts / Extends

- [[cheong-penalizing-transparency-2025]] and [[raj-disclosure-penalty-2026]] provide related disclosure evidence; evaluation outcomes and designs differ.
- The experiments show that transparency can carry a trust cost in these settings. They do not resolve whether disclosure is ethically required or desirable overall.
- The authors relate the findings to conflict-of-interest disclosure research; similarity does not establish a single universal mechanism.

## Open Questions

- The legitimacy mechanism predicts that as norms shift toward expecting AI involvement, the penalty should fade. Their familiarity comparison does not measure change in the penalty over time. Will follow-up studies in 2027–2028 show movement?
- Does the penalty differ by *what* the AI did (drafted vs. edited vs. fact-checked vs. translated)? The disclosure manipulations in this paper are mostly binary (used / didn't use); a more granular dose-response would clarify which AI involvements specifically violate legitimacy.
- For collaborative work with explicit AI co-authorship norms (e.g., software engineering), is there a sub-domain where the penalty inverts?
