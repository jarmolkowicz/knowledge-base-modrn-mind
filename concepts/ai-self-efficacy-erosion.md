# AI Self-Efficacy Erosion

## What It Is

In Gabbiadini et al.'s evaluative settings, AI-versus-human evaluation reduced reported self-efficacy and free-will beliefs and increased self-objectification. These are short-term self-perception outcomes, distinct from measured loss of skill.

## Why It Matters

Self-efficacy is not just confidence — it is the belief that you can organize and execute actions to achieve goals. When AI interactions erode this belief, people don't just feel less capable; they begin to see themselves as objects (data points, resources) rather than agents. This is a distinct erosion pathway from [[cognitive-debt]] or [[confidence-competence-gap]] — it attacks not skill but self-perception.

## Key Insight

Across three experiments (total N=571), measured indirect paths were consistent with a proposed sequence from lower self-efficacy through self-objectification to reduced free-will beliefs. The mediators were not independently randomized, so the complete causal chain was not isolated. One experiment used an actual recruiting system; lasting effects remain untested.

## Evidence

- **Pilot + Study 1** (scenario-based): Participants imagining AI-evaluated job interviews reported lower self-efficacy and higher self-objectification than those imagining human interviews
- **Study 2** (real AI system): Participants interacting with an actual AI recruiting tool showed the same pattern with stronger ecological validity
- **Mediation**: Self-efficacy reduction mediated the relationship between AI interaction and self-objectification; self-objectification mediated the path to reduced free will beliefs
- **Sample**: ~571 Italian participants across three studies

**Counterpoint — Han et al. (2025)**: Han et al. (2025) found positive associations between reported AI use, self-efficacy and willingness to take risks in a three-wave employee survey (N=442). Learning goal orientation moderated the model, but a nonsignificant indirect association at low orientation does not establish shallow confidence or absence of genuine capability. Actual risk-taking, unaided skill and attribution of success were not measured. Comparing workplace tool use with Gabbiadini et al.'s AI-evaluation experiments suggests a question about interaction mode, not a tested explanation for different results.

Hermann, Puntoni & Morewedge (2025), in an Opinion article, discuss self-efficacy erosion in the broader competence-need frustration of Self-Determination Theory: GenAI's "creative, analytical, and communicative affordances can foster a sense of professional inadequacy and make workers feel less competent" [p.4]. The review proposes two possible mechanisms beyond the evaluative-context pathway documented by Gabbiadini et al. — (a) **comparison threat**: GenAI's visible capability on tasks workers used to perform well makes their own contributions feel diminished, even when those contributions remain objectively valuable; and (b) **deskilling-by-offloading**: as workers outsource tasks to GenAI without fully mastering them, the foundational skills that ground self-efficacy beliefs erode, eventually leaving workers unable to perform without AI assistance. [Inference] This raises questions related to [[performance-paradox]] and [[cognitive-debt]], but felt competence is not the same outcome as unaided exam performance or neural measures. Bastani's same-session results do not demonstrate lasting skill loss or this subjective pathway.

Wu et al. (2025) examined short two-task sequences in four experiments (total N=3,562). In Study 4, Solo→Collab participants reported a control decrease of −1.01 (d=.84), while Collab→Solo participants reported an increase of .42 (reported d=−.39). Control remained stable during sustained collaboration. These are perceived-control outcomes, not self-efficacy or lasting agency loss. The study did not test whether Hermann et al.'s proposed comparison threat explains the pattern.
