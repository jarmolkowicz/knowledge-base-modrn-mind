# AI, Self-Determination, and Competence Authenticity

## Citation

Frenzel-Piasentin, A., Finze, N., & Kosok, M. (2026). “You Don't Have To Use What The AI Suggests” — A Self-Determination Perspective On Artificial Intelligence In Knowledge Work. *ECIS 2026 Proceedings*, 8. [Conference record](https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2026/resp_AI/resp_AI/8).

## Type

Completed conference research paper; exploratory qualitative study.

## Key Insight

AI can improve output while changing whether workers experience that output as grounded in their own skill. The authors call this [[competence-authenticity]]: felt alignment between results and personal mastery, understanding, and effort.

## Methods and Evidence

Five online focus groups, June–July 2024, involved 22 participants in marketing, IT, and finance. Participants included employees, interns, and working students; AI use ranged from several times monthly to daily. Sessions lasted 56–72 minutes. Recruitment used personal and professional networks. An iterative three-stage coding process moved from first-order codes through categories to aggregate dimensions (PDF pp.5–7).

Six themes described both support and frustration of basic psychological needs: independence, dependency, competence shifts, competence authenticity, interaction enhancement, and alienation. Participants reported learning and saved time alongside reliance without understanding, unease about authorship, and fewer informal exchanges. These are accounts of experience, not measured changes in skill or causal estimates (pp.8–12).

## Key Passages

> “More specifically, competence authenticity refers to the perceived alignment between performance outcomes and one’s own mastery, understanding, and effort.”
> — Frenzel-Piasentin, Finze, & Kosok, [p.10], section 4.4

> “As a result, knowledge workers tend to either overestimate or underestimate their own capabilities”
> — Frenzel-Piasentin, Finze, & Kosok, [p.10], section 4.4

## Relevance

Adds workers' own experience to the KB's distinction between output and capability. A worker can overcredit AI-supported success to personal ability, or feel illegitimate even after minor editing assistance. Those experiences require different responses.

## Supports

- [[basic-psychological-needs]] — satisfaction and frustration of autonomy, competence and relatedness; not a measure of retained skill.

- [[competence-authenticity]] — named contribution, grounded in focus-group accounts.
- [[confidence-competence-gap]] — reports success without understanding, while also showing possible underestimation.
- [[professional-identity-threat]] — participants questioned the personal value of their contributions.
- [[agency]] — choice, mastery, and social connection shape experienced self-direction.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Extends [[hermann-genai-psychology-work-2025]] with qualitative accounts of the three psychological needs and a specific perceived-origin construct.
- Distinguishes [[authenticity]] as others' evaluation of work from a worker's experience of their own mastery. Neither perception establishes actual competence.

## Limitations

Small, network-recruited sample across three domains; heterogeneous tools and experience. Focus groups cannot establish prevalence, causation, or the proposed transition from independence to dependency over time. The authors' process model and suggested training interventions need longitudinal or experimental tests. “Social friction” here describes workplace frustration, not exactly the developmental construct in [[social-friction]].

## Open Questions

- Can competence authenticity be measured separately from objective ability and confidence?
- Which forms of assistance preserve understanding while reducing unwarranted feelings of cheating?
