# Singh Yadav (2026) — The AI Competency Paradox

## Citation

Singh Yadav, P. (2026). *The AI Competency Paradox: Why AI Doesn't Replace Jobs, but Reshapes Organizational Competence*. Springer (Future of Business and Finance series). ISBN 978-3-032-11748-9.

**DOI:** [10.1007/978-3-032-11748-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-11748-9)

## Type

Book (322 pages; theoretical synthesis with case studies; Springer Future of Business and Finance series)

## Key Insight

Singh Yadav proposes an AI-Competence Ceiling: assistance might support current performance while reducing practice needed for later proficiency. This is a Dreyfus-based developmental hypothesis, not a measured ceiling.

The book distinguishes intrinsic domain expertise from extrinsic skills in directing and evaluating AI. It calls assisted performance that exceeds unaided competence an “augmentation illusion,” and proposes an AI Competency Erosion Index (ACEI). The index is not validated.

The claim of a 40–60% performance advantage for people strong in both competency types is reproduced below as an author claim (p.23). The book's cited basis has not been independently verified and the figure should not be treated as an established estimate. Its historical and company examples are illustrative secondary accounts, not causal tests of the competence-ceiling hypothesis.

## Key Passages

> "Intrinsic competencies represent domain-specific expertise developed through experience: clinical judgment in healthcare, strategic insight in business, and creative problem-solving across disciplines. These capabilities provide contextual understanding, pattern recognition, and judgment that remain difficult for AI systems to replicate."
> — Singh Yadav, Ch.1, [p.23]

> "Extrinsic competencies involve the ability to effectively leverage AI and other technological tools: directing algorithms, interpreting outputs, integrating insights, and maintaining oversight. These skills didn't exist in traditional professional development but now determine competitive advantage."
> — Singh Yadav, Ch.1, [p.23]

> "Research across professional services firms shows that individuals strong in both competency types achieve 40–60% higher performance than those excelling in only one dimension. However, most professionals lack systematic development in extrinsic competencies, while AI implementation often erodes intrinsic capabilities."
> — Singh Yadav, Ch.1, [p.23]

> "Chapter 4 introduces the AI-Competence Ceiling hypothesis, demonstrating how AI's selective amplification effects disrupt the natural progression from competent to expert-level performance described in the Dreyfus model."

> "Production targets were missed by 83% in the first quarter after launch (planned, 5000 units/week; actual, 793 units/week). Quality control defects increased by 340% compared to previous model production."
> — Singh Yadav, Ch.1.4, [p.24] (the author's Tesla example; numerical claims reproduced as source claims, not independently verified here)

## Methodology

Theoretical synthesis with case study analysis. Draws on:
- Dreyfus & Dreyfus expertise model
- Ericsson deliberate practice literature
- Porter / Teece strategy frameworks (applied to AI adoption)
- Case studies (Tesla vs Toyota manufacturing, professional services firms)

No primary empirical data; the contribution is integrative.

## Relevance

Provides an organizational framing for distinguishing assisted performance, domain expertise and AI-use skills. Its contribution is theoretical synthesis. Neither the claimed developmental ceiling nor ACEI supplies an established diagnostic threshold.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Synthesizes [[bjork-desirable-difficulties-2011]], [[ericsson-deliberate-practice-1993]], [[hatano-inagaki-adaptive-expertise-1986]] into an organizational framework. The synthesis does not independently test those findings or their application to AI.
- ACEI as an unvalidated measurement framework: proposes indicators; their validity remains untested.

## Open Questions

- The AI-Competence Ceiling hypothesis is theoretically motivated but not directly empirically tested. Distinguishing "AI prevents progression past competent" from "AI raises the floor while top experts are unchanged" requires longitudinal individual-level data the book doesn't present.
- ACEI methodology: the index is proposed but not validated. What measurement instruments? What benchmarks? Open research program.
- Dreyfus model has been challenged in cognitive-science literature (overly stage-based; experts can be flexibly novice in unfamiliar contexts). The book's reliance on Dreyfus inherits those concerns.
- Industry / role specificity: the case studies are mostly manufacturing and professional services. Generalization to other domains (creative work, research, healthcare frontline) is asserted but not directly demonstrated.
