# Marcoccia et al. (2026) — AI Advice and Saying “I Don’t Know”

## Citation

Marcoccia, C., Quattrociocchi, W., & Capraro, V. (2026). *AI advice suppresses people’s willingness to say “I don’t know”, even when the advice is wrong and accuracy is incentivized*. Preprint, [arXiv:2607.13562v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13562v1), submitted 15 July 2026. The record matches the supplied authors, title, five-experiment design and N=3,132. Materials address printed in the paper: https://osf.io/nwx6r/

## Type

Preprint — five experiments; four preregistered and one direct replication. Primary preprint record checked on 26 September 2026; this does not establish peer review.

## Key Insight

Access to usually wrong AI suggestions sharply reduced the choice to withhold an answer. Small accuracy incentives partly restored restraint and accuracy but did not remove the AI-related difference. The distinctive outcome is willingness to answer at all, rather than correctness alone.

## Evidence

- Five experiments, N=3,132 (314, 310, 812, 843, 853), using six film-detail questions selected to induce errors from Step 3.5 Flash. Study 1a used live responses; subsequent studies displayed randomly selected prerecorded model answers. This is a deliberately unreliable-advice setting, not an estimate of general AI accuracy (PDF pp.3–4, 14–20).
- Participants could always decline. Authors coded blanks, explicit ignorance and nonresponsive entries as suspension; hedged substantive guesses counted as answers. This operationalization is broader than a literal “I don’t know” response (p.15).
- Suspension proportions without versus with optional AI: .36 versus .06 in Study 1a; .44 versus .03 in Study 1b. Study 4 displayed AI suggestions automatically: suspension was .35 versus .01 without stakes and .39 versus .07 with stakes (pp.4, 10).
- Studies 2–4 offered $0.10 per correct response, −$0.10 per incorrect response and zero for suspension. Studies 3–4 repeated the incentive before every question. The preregistered AI-by-stakes interaction on suspension was nonsignificant in all three studies. Stakes had some independent benefits, rather than demonstrably weakening the AI effect (pp.6, 8, 10).
- In Study 2 without stakes, confidence averaged 29.6 without AI versus 75.9 with AI on a 0–100 scale, while correctness fell from .28 to .10. Confidence and correctness analyses were exploratory (pp.6, 18).

## Key Passages

> "Importantly, our goal is not to estimate the overall accuracy of AI assistants, but to isolate the behavioral effect of making an answer available when that answer is systematically unreliable."
> — Marcoccia et al., PDF p.3

> "Second, our design did not record whether participants who overrode the AI consulted other sources, such as the open Internet or another model, which limits inferences about the strategies behind successful overrides."
> — Marcoccia et al., PDF p.13

## Relevance

Adds a directly measured restraint decision to [[metacognition]] and [[cognitive-surrender]]. [Inference] Evaluation should measure appropriate refusal or uncertainty alongside the percentage of answers correct.

## Supports

- [[metacognition]] — answer availability changes the decision to volunteer an answer.
- [[cognitive-surrender]] — extends error-following results with a separate suspension outcome.
- [[confidence-competence-gap]] — confidence rose while correctness fell in the tested setting.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Extends [[shaw-cognitive-surrender-2026]] by measuring suspension explicitly; this is not merely another accuracy comparison.
- Qualifies a broad claim that incentives cannot help: accuracy improved and restraint sometimes increased, although the preregistered moderation hypothesis failed.

## Limitations

Six questions from one narrow domain, one deliberately unreliable advice source and modest monetary stakes. Coders were authors and blinding is not reported. No direct verification logs or test of competing mechanisms: trust, response cost and fluency remain explanations to test. These experiments do not demonstrate durable loss of cognitive capacity.

## Open Questions

- Does the suspension effect persist with mostly correct advice, domain expertise or consequential decisions?
- Can explicit uncertainty options or better verification improve decisions?
- Reporting inconsistencies to resolve: “nearly doubled” confidence in the abstract versus 29.6→75.9 in Study 2; p.10 says accuracy improves when AI is “unavailable” immediately after numbers showing the benefit with AI; p.6 and p.10 give different p-values for Study 2's null suspension interaction. Draft retains the directly reported means and the consistent null conclusion, not those conflicting labels.
