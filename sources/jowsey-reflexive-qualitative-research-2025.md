# A Methodological Boundary for Reflexive Qualitative Research

## Citation

Jowsey, T., Braun, V., Clarke, V., Lupton, D., & Fine, M. (2025). We Reject the Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence for Reflexive Qualitative Research. *Qualitative Inquiry*, advance online publication, 1–5. [Publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1177/10778004251401851), first published 17 December 2025.

The citation year follows the published article (2025).

## Type

Methodological position article signed by five named authors and endorsed by others: 419 researchers in total, from 32 countries. Not an empirical comparison or a survey of all qualitative researchers.

## Key Insight

The authors argue that reflexive qualitative analysis requires a situated human interpreter, making AI-generated coding incompatible with the method even when a human checks it. Their objection concerns what constitutes the analysis, not only the accuracy of a finished output.

## Argument and Evidence

The full substantive argument appears on pp.1–2. It concerns Big Q approaches such as reflexive thematic analysis and phenomenology. Three grounds support the authors' rejection: statistical prediction lacks their required meaning-making; reflexive inquiry is a human practice involving subjectivity and power; and AI production/use raises environmental and labor-justice concerns. They distinguish this work from count-based content analysis, which can be automated.

Endorsements were solicited through professional networks and social media for ten days from 13 October 2025 (p.4). This documents support for a position, not its prevalence or an experimental result. Environmental and labor claims rely on cited literature rather than measurements made in this article.

## Key Passages

> “Qualitative research should remain a distinctly human practice;”
> — Jowsey et al., [p.1], statement of reasons

> “use of GenAI is inappropriate in all phases of reflexive qualitative analysis, including initial coding.”
> — Jowsey et al., [p.2], statement of their position

> “We kept the invitation open for 10 days before closing it.”
> — Jowsey et al., [p.4], endorsement procedure

## Relevance

Adds a specific boundary case for [[judgment]]: in some methods, the interpreter's situated reasoning is part of the work being claimed. A review step alone may not preserve that methodological commitment.

## Supports

- [[judgment]] — interpretation depends on context, standpoint, and responsibility.
- [[calibration]] — task allocation needs a methodological-fit check as well as an output-quality check.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Qualifies [[calibration]]: “AI drafts, human reviews” is not accepted by these authors for reflexive analysis, including initial coding.
- Extends concerns in [[scientific-monoculture]] about reproducing dominant patterns, as an explicit methodological argument rather than new empirical evidence of homogenization.

## Limitations

This is a normative and epistemological position. Endorsements do not prove that every qualitative method must exclude AI, or that every administrative research task is in scope. The article does not experimentally establish skill loss, biased outputs, or superior human analysis. Its claims about meaning-making and its broader ethical objections should remain attributed to the authors rather than presented as universal settled facts.

## Open Questions

- How should teams distinguish reflexive interpretation from descriptive coding, retrieval, transcription, and other support tasks?
- What contrary methodological arguments and outcome studies should accompany this position in a broader review?
