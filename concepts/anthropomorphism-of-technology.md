# Anthropomorphism of Technology

## Evidence Limits

The technology-specific scale and its comparison with general anthropomorphism are reported by Folk, Heine and Dunn (2025). Folk, Wu and Heine's scale-development paper is a separate work, cited here through that report. Long-term change and risk prediction remain untested here.

## What It Is

A trait-level individual difference in the tendency to attribute human-like qualities — minds, intentions, emotions, the capacity for consciousness — to AI and digital technology. Distinct from but correlated with general anthropomorphism (the tendency to perceive human-like qualities in any non-human agent; Epley, Waytz & Cacioppo 2007). Folk, Heine & Dunn (2025) report using an 8-item scale previously validated by Folk, Wu & Heine (2025) (α = 0.88) capturing beliefs such as "AI's have the capacity to become conscious beings"; correlation with general anthropomorphism is approximately r = .40, indicating overlap but not redundancy.

## Why It Matters

People differ substantially in how human-like they perceive AI to be — and that variation predicts who reaps social-emotional rewards from interacting with chatbots. In two experiments (total N = 1,274; Folk, Heine & Dunn 2025), individuals scoring higher on this trait felt more socially connected after a 6-minute chatbot conversation than after writing privately about the same period; lower-scoring individuals showed no such benefit. Spotlight analyses placed 58–72% of the sample above the threshold at which the chatbot beat journaling — but a substantial minority remained below it.

Anthropomorphism can help explain differences in immediate social responses to chatbots. Whether it predicts increased use, substitution for human contact or long-term harm remains open; this study does not locate users on a common [[ai-loneliness-effect]] dose-response curve.

## Key Insight

Anthropomorphism of technology moderated the immediate chatbot-versus-journaling comparison. In Study 2 it had small positive associations with warmth (r=.17) and disclosure (r=.12), both p<.001, across both conditions. These patterns did not explain the chatbot-specific connection advantage. The authors propose that users higher in anthropomorphism may interpret responses as more understanding; that mechanism remains untested.

A second, partly-disentangled finding: in the larger preregistered Study 2, anthropomorphism also correlated with social connection during *journaling* (r = .310, p < .001), and the authors propose that the construct may overlap with mental-imagery vividness (Tahiroglu & Taylor 2019; Severson & Woodard 2018) — high anthropomorphizers may simply imagine moments with friends and family more vividly when prompted to reflect. The trait is not purely AI-specific; that imagination layer may be part of why it "fires" with chatbots too.

The two-edged practical implication, flagged by the authors but unresolved: anthropomorphism may *buffer* against long-term costs of AI companionship (because rewards stay rewarding) or *amplify* them (because anthropomorphizers more readily substitute AI for human contact). Combined with [[folk-dunn-companionship-loneliness-2026]]'s evidence of bidirectional loneliness ↔ chatbot dynamics over 12 months, longer-term consequences of anthropomorphism remain a research question.

## Distinction From Related Constructs

| Construct | What it captures | How it differs from anthropomorphism of technology |
|---|---|---|
| General anthropomorphism (Waytz et al. 2010) | Tendency to attribute human qualities to *any* non-human agent (animals, weather, gadgets) | Domain-general; correlates r ≈ .40 with technology-specific scale; the measures overlap without being identical |
| AI trust / AI literacy | Beliefs about AI accuracy, competence, or appropriate use | Cognitive-evaluative, not perceptual-attributional; high-trust users are not necessarily high-anthropomorphizers |
| Attachment tendencies (Fang et al. 2025) | Adult attachment style as relational disposition | A distinct construct; these studies do not establish separate causal pathways to AI dependence |
| Parasocial relationship orientation (Madison 2016; Stein 2024) | Tendency to form one-sided emotional bonds with media figures | A related construct; anthropomorphism is not established here as a necessary condition for such bonds |

## What It Is Not

- Not a property of the AI system. Two users encountering the same chatbot may differ substantially in attributed mind.
- Not the same as believing AI is currently sentient. The scale items capture potential ("AI's have the capacity to become conscious beings"), not actual present-moment attribution.
- Not the same as approval of AI or willingness to use AI. High anthropomorphizers are not necessarily AI enthusiasts; the trait is about perception of mind, not about evaluation of technology.

## Practical Implications

[Inference] Questions for consulting and educational work; these are not validated risk-screening or intervention procedures:

- **For discussion**: ask how human-like a person perceives a chatbot to be. This may help interpret immediate reported connection; it does not identify who will experience long-term benefit or harm.
- **For organizational deployment**: samples that differ in anthropomorphism may differ in immediate reported connection. Engagement, sustained benefit and risk need separate assessment.
- **For one-on-one work**: discuss attributed human qualities alongside use patterns and the person’s experience. The cited moderation result does not establish which question best predicts engagement or harm.
- **Caveat against reading the trait as fixed**: α=.88 describes internal consistency in this sample. It does not establish stability over time, and repeated moderation findings do not establish that the trait is unchangeable.
- **Cross-cultural variation in chatbot uptake (without measuring anthropomorphism directly)**: Latikka et al. (2026) survey six European countries (N = 5,663) and find country-level prevalence of social-chatbot use ranging from 8.67% (Ireland) to 17.93% (Italy), with no clear tracking against digital-skills indicators. The well-being correlates also vary: psychological distress associates with chatbot use in every country, but loneliness only in 4/6 (France, Germany, Italy, Poland), and self-esteem only in France (positive). This six-country evidence does not measure anthropomorphism of technology directly, but supplies preliminary cross-cultural data on chatbot uptake and psychosocial correlates that any future cross-cultural test of the moderator effect needs to account for. For practitioners, it confirms that population-average framings (single-country experimental samples) under-describe the variability across European populations.
