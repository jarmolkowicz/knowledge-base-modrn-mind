# Zhao & He (2024) — AI Augmentation and Workplace Impostor Thoughts

## Citation

Zhao, P., & He, G. (2024). The impostor phenomenon of workplace artificial intelligence augmentation. *Academy of Management Proceedings*. https://doi.org/10.5465/AMPROC.2024.148bp

## Type

Short conference proceedings paper describing three experiments and a three-wave survey. It is not a full journal report; references are available from the authors rather than supplied here.

## Key Insight

AI-assisted success can feel difficult to claim as one's own. Zhao and He find increased impostor thoughts after AI augmentation, especially among workers who value performing the work for its own sake; the downstream links to coworker behavior are observational.

## Evidence

- **Study 1:** 184 US employees recruited for a randomized 200-word writing task, with or without permission to use ChatGPT. Assignment to AI access increased impostor thoughts (reported d = 0.43, p < .01). An instrumental-variable analysis also supported an effect under imperfect compliance. Recruitment and the reported t-test degrees of freedom differ; the short report does not fully explain attrition.
- **Study 2:** 170 Chinese employees recruited for randomized recall of work done with an LLM or independently. Impostor thoughts were higher after AI recall (d = 0.52). Measured intrinsic motivation moderated this difference. Recall assignment is not an experiment on sustained workplace adoption.
- **Study 3:** 170 Chinese employees recruited for a 2 × 2 scenario experiment crossing image-AI augmentation and intrinsic motivation. The reported augmentation-by-motivation interaction was positive. A sentence calls the low-motivation slope non-significant while giving p < .001; that subgroup conclusion is unresolved.
- **Study 4:** final sample of 210 US employees, with surveys one week apart. AI augmentation and motivation were measured first, impostor thoughts second, and self-reported knowledge sharing and helping third. The indirect associations were negative for knowledge sharing (−0.030, 95% CI [−0.063, −0.007]) and interpersonal citizenship behavior (−0.035, CI [−0.072, −0.006]).

The study counts sum to 734, but combine recruited samples in Studies 1–3 with the retained sample in Study 4. Do not describe 734 as a verified pooled analyzed N.

## Relevance

This supplies a specific account of [[professional-identity-threat]]: feeling fraudulent after success, rather than simply disliking automation or believing that one's skills are weak. It also identifies a possible social consequence of that experience. It does not show that AI users are fraudulent, that intrinsic motivation should be reduced, or that all augmentation undermines identity.

## Contradicts / Extends

- Extends: [[hai-dark-side-collaboration-2025]] — links identity-related experience with coworker contribution through a different observational pathway.

## Open Questions

- Can attribution of one's human contribution reduce impostor thoughts? This intervention was not tested.
- Would the effect persist in long-term work with verified performance and colleague reports?
- Study 3's low-motivation result conflicts with its printed p-value. Some standard-deviation labels and sample reporting also need clarification.
- Does the full journal version, if one exists, resolve these reporting issues? The supplied proceedings text alone cannot answer.
