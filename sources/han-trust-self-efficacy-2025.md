# Han, Song, Zhang & Li (2025) — Trust the Machine or Trust Yourself

## Citation

Han, Z., Song, G., Zhang, Y., & Li, B. (2025). Trust the Machine or Trust Yourself: How AI Usage Reshapes Employee Self-Efficacy and Willingness to Take Risks. *Behavioral Sciences*, 15(8), 1046.

**DOI:** [10.3390/bs15081046](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs15081046)

## Type

Paper (three-wave longitudinal survey, N=442 Chinese employees, moderated mediation model)

## Key Insight

A three-wave survey of 442 Chinese employees found positive associations between reported AI use, self-efficacy and willingness to take risks. These are self-reported outcomes, not observed risk-taking or tests of unaided competence.

The reported AI-use coefficients were B = 0.524 for self-efficacy and B = 0.493 for willingness to take risks (both p < .001). The model included an indirect association through self-efficacy (0.122, 95% CI [0.071, 0.176]). Learning goal orientation moderated the model: the conditional indirect association was significant at high orientation but not at low orientation.

The nonsignificant low-orientation result does not demonstrate shallow confidence, external attribution of success, lack of genuine capability or absence of behavioural change. Those were not measured. The survey's temporal separation does not establish causation.

## Key Passages

> "AI usage significantly enhances employees' willingness to take risks; … self-efficacy serves as a partial mediator in the connection between AI usage and the willingness to take risks; … learning goal orientation moderates both the relationship between AI usage and self-efficacy, as well as the mediating effect."
> — Han et al., [p.1] (abstract)

> "When employees use AI to assist in decision-making, the data analysis and predictive capabilities provided by the system may reduce uncertainty, thereby increasing their confidence in taking risks."
> — Han et al., [p.3]

> "Said et al. (2023) demonstrated that individuals with greater confidence in AI knowledge tend to amplify potential benefits while underestimating risks, which may enhance employees' willingness to take risks in AI-assisted decision-making contexts. This cognitive bias toward optimistic risk assessment suggests that AI familiarity creates a psychological environment conducive to bolder decision-making."
> — Han et al., [p.4]

> "When employees become proficient in using AI tools and receive positive feedback, they may attribute this success to an enhancement of their abilities, thereby increasing their self-efficacy."
> — Han et al., [p.4]

## Methodology

- Three-wave survey of 442 Chinese employees; observational, not randomized AI assignment.
- Moderated mediation analysis with bootstrapped confidence intervals.
- Self-efficacy: ten-item scale from Scholz et al. (2002).
- Willingness to take risks: eight self-report items adapted from Dewett (2006), not observed risky decisions.
- AI usage and learning goal orientation were also reported. No unaided skill assessment or direct measure of attribution was included.

## Relevance

The study shows why confidence, willingness and demonstrated capability should be kept separate. Its associations motivate questions about learning orientation, but do not validate an intervention to increase it or show whether higher confidence is well calibrated.

[Inference] Comparing AI use as a work tool with AI-based evaluation may help frame future research. Contrasting this survey with [[alessandro-self-efficacy-2025]] does not isolate interaction mode as the cause of different findings. Quoted causal language in Key Passages reflects the authors' interpretation, not a randomized design.

## Contradicts / Extends

- [[alessandro-self-efficacy-2025]] studies being evaluated by AI. Its design, population and task differ; the two papers do not establish a general tool-versus-evaluator rule.
- [[passalacqua-less-ai-2024]] compares automation conditions. Han's survey does not replicate that intervention or show that learning orientation prevents automation-related harms.

## Open Questions

- Do these associations generalize beyond the sampled employees and setting?
- Does changing learning orientation or AI use cause changes in willingness, actual decisions or skill?
- How do people attribute AI-assisted success, and does that attribution predict later unaided performance?
- Are self-efficacy changes well calibrated, and how long do they persist?
