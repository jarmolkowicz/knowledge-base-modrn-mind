# Cognitive Surrender

## What It Is

Shaw & Nave's term for uncritical adoption of AI advice. Their theory contrasts it with strategic [[cognitive-offloading]], where users evaluate external support. The experiments operationalize follow/override behavior; they do not directly show that all internal reasoning has stopped.

## Why It Matters

Across three experiments (N=1,372; 9,593 trials), 73.2% of AI-engaged faulty trials involved following the faulty answer, 19.7% successful overrides and 7.1% failed overrides. Study 1's faulty-advice acceptance rate was 79.8%. These conditional trial rates are not prevalence across all AI use. Confidence and accuracy changed under the tested conditions; long-term skill effects were not measured.

## Key Insight

The proposed account concerns how reliance can shift toward an external system. Treat bypassed deliberation as the authors' theoretical explanation, not a directly measured location or transfer of cognition.

Three factors predict greater surrender:
- Higher trust in AI (OR = 4.36 for surrender over offloading)
- Lower need for cognition
- Lower fluid intelligence

Two findings in the tested tasks, not proof of long-term protection:
- Performance incentives combined with immediate feedback (override of faulty AI doubled from ~20% to ~42%)
- Higher cognitive reflection ability

Chiriatti et al. (2024) provide a complementary theoretical account from outside the experimental paradigm. In their *system 0* framework, surrender becomes the default because the AI substrate satisfies Heersmink's "trust" criterion at the cognitive-extension level — its outputs are accepted, in their words, "with the same ease and readiness with which we accept perceptual input or the content of our memory." This reframes the predictors Shaw & Nave identify (high trust in AI, low need for cognition, low fluid intelligence) as individual variation in how *tightly* a user has coupled with system 0; the structural pull toward surrender is built into the substrate, not solely into the moment-of-decision dynamics.

## Surrender vs. Offloading vs. Automation Bias

The table summarizes theoretical distinctions; its System 2 and agency rows were not directly measured as states in the experiments.

| | Cognitive Surrender | [[cognitive-offloading]] | [[automation-bias]] |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What** | Abdication of reasoning | Strategic delegation | Error of trust |
| **System 2** | Bypassed | Active (integrates AI output) | Active but miscalibrated |
| **Agency** | Transferred to AI | Retained by user | Retained but impaired |
| **Scope** | Broad epistemic dependence | Task-specific | Error-specific |

## Extension Beyond Cognitive Tasks

Sharma et al. (2026) examined 1.5 million Claude.ai conversations for potential and apparent actualized disempowerment. Their action-distortion category includes users seeking ready-to-use messages or plans for value-laden personal decisions. These observations concern conversation content, not a direct measure of internal deliberation, transferred agency or Shaw and Nave's experimental construct.

The study reports apparent actualized action distortion in approximately 0.018% of sampled conversations, based on conversation-level signals such as reported action followed by regret. This is not a verified rate of interpersonal harm among unique users, nor evidence that all scripted assistance involves cognitive surrender.

[Inference] Advice-following in laboratory tasks and delegation visible in conversations may raise related questions about independent judgment. Their constructs, denominators and methods differ; neither study establishes one universal mechanism or a long-term loss of capability.

## Workplace-Behavior Extension

Hai et al. (2025) studied daily associations among GenAI collaboration, work alienation and self-reported expediency (229 employees; 1,050 observations). Collaboration–alienation and alienation–expediency coefficients were γ=.14 and γ=.30. Cognitive surrender was not measured, and causal direction is unestablished.

[Inference] These field associations raise questions about responsibility and engagement, but do not show that the same mechanism underlies faulty-advice acceptance, conversation-level disempowerment and employee expediency. The designs, constructs and time horizons differ.

The indirect association in Hai et al. was significant under high digital job demands but not low demands. Low demands do not establish a well-resourced or protective workplace; neither a resource intervention nor a causal surrender-as-expediency mechanism was tested.
