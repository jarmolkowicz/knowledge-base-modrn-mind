# Huffstadt (2026) — The Silent Impact of AI

## Citation

Huffstadt, K. (2026). The Silent Impact of AI: Unveiling Motivational Side Effects in the Digital Workplace. *Intelligent Human Systems Integration (IHSI)*, Vol. 200, 351–361.

**DOI:** [10.54941/ahfe1007094](https://doi.org/10.54941/ahfe1007094)

## Type

Paper (cross-sectional SEM study, N=297 knowledge workers using AI in informatics tasks)

## Key Insight

Huffstadt tests a proposed motivational model using a cross-sectional survey of 297 knowledge workers. Greater AI usage was associated with lower perceived social presence (β=−.42) and effort (β=−.38); motivation was associated with self-reported performance (β=.58). The fitted mediation pattern does not establish an exclusive causal pathway.

Exploratory technological-self-efficacy moderation showed different AI–motivation associations (β=−.33 in the lower group; β=−.12, nonsignificant, in the higher group). It does not establish that self-efficacy training prevents harm.

The study did not measure longitudinal skill loss, unnoticed motivational decline or objective long-term performance. The author's causal language in the excerpts below should be read with these design limits.

## Key Passages

> "AI usage significantly reduces both perceived social presence and cognitive effort, which positively influence motivation. Motivation emerged as the strongest predictor of performance, with full mediation confirmed."
> — Huffstadt, [p.1] (abstract)

> "Even when efficiency improves, the motivational cost may be high if systems erode task meaning or relational depth."
> — Huffstadt, [p.1]

> "When AI automates parts of the task, perceived effort may decrease. Although this can improve efficiency, it can also reduce the psychological value of success. Perceptual fluency - the ease of processing information - can further exacerbate this effect."
> — Huffstadt, [p.3]

> "When users benefit from intelligent systems without understanding them, they may misattribute outcomes to personal ability, distorting feedback and weakening the learning process. In complex tasks, such misperceptions can reduce adaptive growth and long-term performance."
> — Huffstadt, [p.3]

> "Technological self-efficacy moderated the link between AI usage and motivation, suggesting individual resilience factors play a key role."
> — Huffstadt, [p.1]

## Methodology

- N = 297 professionals using AI tools regularly in informatics tasks
- Variables (validated instruments): AI usage; perceived social presence (Short et al.); cognitive effort (Westbrook et al.); motivation (Locke & Latham scale); performance (self-report); technological self-efficacy
- Analysis: structural equation modeling (SEM) with bootstrapped mediation testing
- Theoretical core: High-Performance Cycle (Locke & Latham 2002) + perceptual fluency (Reber et al.) + Dunning-Kruger

## Relevance

The study raises questions about effort, social presence and motivation in AI-supported work. [Inference] These may be useful dimensions to monitor, but neither a skill-preservation intervention nor a general motivational cost of offloading was tested.

## Open Questions

- Cross-sectional design: directionality of AI→motivation isn't established. Could low-motivation workers self-select into heavy AI use (reverse causation).
- "Silent" framing claims users don't notice the erosion. Self-report doesn't measure this directly. Behavioral measures (task disengagement, switching, abandonment) would be stronger evidence.
- Generalization beyond informatics tasks: would the SEM coefficients hold in creative work, customer-facing roles, or routine non-informatics tasks?
