# Update: Disclosure Penalty

## Source

[[claessens-ai-outsourcing-perceptions-2026]]

## Proposed Replacements

Replace the general framing below; do not append a qualification beneath a universal claim. Preserve the existing studies' scoped results and citations. This revision does not re-audit their original datasets or change the entry's status: the supported claim is that penalties occur in studied settings, not that they occur everywhere.

### Replace "What It Is"

A disclosure penalty is a lower evaluation associated with revealing or labeling AI involvement in the settings studied. The outcome may be trust, perceived competence or a work rating. An AI-label effect on identical content is distinct from judging how much work was delegated or whether use was acknowledged; these comparisons need not produce the same pattern.

### Replace "Why It Matters"

Disclosure can carry social costs in studied settings. Existing evidence covers evaluations of creative work, professional scenarios, messages and advice. These findings do not establish a universal penalty on every trait, nor do evaluation ratings alone demonstrate actual career or relationship losses.

### In "Key Insight": replace the opening paragraph

Schilke and Reimann (2025), as summarized in [[reimann-schilke-disclosure-2025]], report trust reductions across 13 experiments spanning several tasks and actor roles, including tests of softer disclosure wording. Those results concern the tested settings and disclosures; they do not establish that all assistance or all forms of acknowledgment reduce trust. Use the published author order shown in that source entry; its legacy slug is unchanged.

### In "Key Insight": qualify generalizations while retaining the reported results

- Replace "provide the largest evidence base to date" in the Raj paragraph with "report a large creative-writing evidence base".
- Replace "The penalty is mediated by perceived authenticity" in that paragraph with "Perceived authenticity statistically mediated evaluations in their analyses".
- Replace the Meincke opening sentence with: "Meincke, Nave and Terwiesch (2026) examine source disclosure in ethical advice."
- Replace its closing sentence "The penalty appears even where transparency norms are strongest." with: "This extends the evidence to ethical-advice evaluations; transparency norms themselves were not ranked or experimentally compared."

Keep those paragraphs' study-specific numerical results, mitigation tests and limitations. Keep Zhu and Molnar's uncertainty/no-information distinction, including the warning that a nonsignificant difference is not equivalence.

### Add one bounded comparison after the existing study paragraphs

Claessens et al. (2026) distinguish assistance with human editing from copying AI output, and acknowledgment from non-acknowledgment. In Study 2, honest assistance had higher pooled morality and trust ratings than the unaided control, alongside greater perceived laziness. Some relational tasks still attracted negative judgments under honest assistance. Full outsourcing generally compared less favorably with assistance. These are task- and trait-sensitive observer judgments, not established career consequences.

### Replace "Worse Than Disclosure" heading and paragraph with "Acknowledgment and delegation"

The existing Schilke and Reimann (2025) source reports greater trust loss when AI use was exposed by a third party than when disclosed voluntarily, in their tested settings. Claessens et al. (2026) separately found that observers rated non-acknowledgment less favorably on morality and trust than acknowledgment. Neither comparison measures the real-world probability of discovering concealed use. These findings must not be combined with AI-label effects into advice to hide AI involvement. The extent of delegation and the honesty of the description are separate questions.
