# Match confidence to the evidence you have

## Use When

A proposal looks persuasive and the team must decide how much to invest.

Author’s intended benefit: Distinguish enthusiasm, opinion, data and tests when judging a proposal.

## Try It

1. List the evidence behind your estimate: conviction, thematic fit, colleague review, customer observations, broader data or an actual test.
2. Separate your enthusiasm from the strength and relevance of that evidence.
3. Choose the next affordable check in proportion to the stakes. A low-risk change may not need more research.
4. If you proceed with uncertainty, define a feedback route that could reveal a wrong assumption, following Tolkin’s variant.
5. [Inference] Treat an AI recommendation as a proposal until you inspect the evidence it uses; fluent prose adds no independent evidence.

## Evidence and Rationale

**Basis:** Practitioner tool explanation and reported field use.

**Observed or reported:** Gilad reports teams use the confidence meter to challenge poorly supported investment; no controlled comparison.

**Related research:** Guingrich, Mehta and Bhatt offer a conceptual account of [[belief-offloading]], not evidence that this routine prevents it. See [[guingrich-belief-offloading-2026]]. This is context, not direct validation.

**Untested:** Whether this specific procedure improves judgment, independent capability, creative quality or calibration beyond the reported experience. A completed artifact, more options and felt clarity are different outcomes.

## Limits

- Confidence-meter numbers are a heuristic, not calibrated probabilities.
- Large datasets and tests can still be biased or poorly designed.
- Some low-consequence changes do not justify additional testing.

## What to Notice

[Inference] Ask what observation would lower confidence. Do not turn the practitioner’s confidence scale into a probability of success.
